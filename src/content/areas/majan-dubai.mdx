Majan is where a specific Dubai sales pattern is most visible, which makes it a useful district to be precise about. Apartments here are marketed on gross yields of 8 to 10 percent, and those figures are accurate. They are also not what the owner receives, and the gap between the two is larger in this kind of community than almost anywhere else in the emirate.

## Where does the gross yield actually go?

Into two lines that a gross figure excludes by definition, and both of them are larger here than in plainer stock.

The first is the service charge. Our underwriting stress-tests gross yield claims of 8 to 10 percent against service charges and one to two months of vacancy, and on family amenity communities the net commonly compresses by 1 to 3 percentage points. On an 8 percent gross that is a net closer to 5 to 7 percent, and the whole of that difference is money that leaves the owner's account every year.

The second is vacancy. A gross yield assumes twelve months of rent. A real hold produces gaps between tenancies, and each gap costs both the empty months and a re-letting commission near 5% of annual rent. One to two months is a conservative and realistic assumption rather than a pessimistic one.

Neither adjustment is a criticism of the community. They are the arithmetic that turns a marketing figure into an investment figure, and the reason to apply them here specifically is that amenity-led communities have a structurally higher first line than plain buildings do. The method for building the number properly is in [the Dubai rental yield guide](/guides/dubai-rental-yield-guide/).

## Why does amenity raise the charge?

Because amenity has to be run, and running it is a recurring cost paid by owners.

A pool needs plant, chemicals, cleaning and supervision. A gym needs equipment that wears out and is replaced. Children's facilities need maintenance, safety compliance and often staffing. Landscaped areas need irrigation and grounds contracts. Shared indoor spaces need cooling, cleaning and security. All of that arrives in the annual service charge, levied per square foot of built area, and it does not fall when occupancy does.

That is a fair exchange when the amenity raises the achievable rent enough to cover it. Family communities often clear that bar, because the facilities are part of why a family chooses the community and part of why they stay. It is a poor exchange when the amenity is extensive relative to what the local rent can support, which is the specific risk in a district where entry pricing is low and the facility provision is marketed heavily.

There is a second-order effect worth naming, because it decides how the charge behaves over a hold rather than in year one. Amenity equipment has a service life. Pool plant, gym equipment, play structures and the systems that support shared spaces all reach replacement at some point, and in a building whose reserve fund was set conservatively that replacement arrives as an increase or a special assessment rather than as a planned draw. A community marketed on the depth of its facilities is therefore carrying a larger future replacement liability than a plainer building of the same age, and the reserve fund position is the only document that shows whether it has been provided for.

The way to tell the difference before buying is to compare the charge per square foot against the rent per square foot for the same building, rather than admiring the facility list. A building where the charge consumes a large share of the rent is a building where the amenity is being funded by the owner rather than by the tenant. Both figures are obtainable before an offer, and comparing them takes minutes, which makes it the cheapest piece of diligence available in this district and the one most often skipped.

## What does Majan cost to buy into?

Entry pricing in the community starts from around AED 800,000, which places it in Dubai's affordable band alongside the other outer Dubailand communities.

That figure is an entry point rather than a typical transaction, and it comes from current project pricing in the community rather than from a market average. A specific unit should be priced against recent transactions of the same size and specification, because in a district still receiving new supply the spread between a launch price and a resale price is real and moves with the delivery cycle. One of the projects in the community is covered in detail on [the Samana Barari Heights page](/projects/samana-barari-heights/).

The transaction stack is the same across the emirate. The illustration below uses AED 800,000.

| --- | --- | --- |
| DLD transfer fee | 4% of price | AED 32,000 |
| Agency commission | 2% plus 5% VAT | AED 16,800 |
| Trustee office fee | Capped by DLD regulation | AED 4,200 |
| Developer no-objection certificate | AED 500 to 5,000 | AED 1,500 |
| Title deed issuance | AED 250 to 580 | Included in the trustee bundle |
| **Total closing** | **6% to 7%** | **about AED 54,500** |

With a mortgage, add registration at 0.25% of the loan, an arrangement fee near 1% and a valuation of AED 2,500 to 3,500, taking the all-in figure closer to 8%. At this price point the entry costs are a substantial share of the capital committed, and a yield calculated against AED 800,000 rather than against roughly AED 855,000 overstates the return before any other adjustment. ## Can a foreign buyer own here?

Yes, outright. Majan sits within designated freehold territory in Dubailand, so title is issued in the buyer's own name with no lease term attached, and the plot-level eligibility check that governs Dubai's older districts does not apply.

- Title is freehold and recorded in the Dubai Land Department register
- Ownership carries no residency requirement, and residency does not follow from ownership
- Payment plans on off-plan purchases are a developer arrangement rather than an ownership question

The designated list is in [the Dubai freehold areas list](/guides/freehold-areas-dubai-list/). Here the legal position is settled, which is precisely why the financial diligence carries the whole weight of the decision.

## An insider tip on stress-testing a yield claim

Insider tip: when an agent quotes a gross yield in an amenity-led community, ask for three numbers instead of arguing with the first one. The service charge per square foot for that specific building, in writing from the owners association. The reserve fund position. And the actual rent achieved on comparable units in that building over the last twelve months, not the asking rents currently advertised. With those three you can rebuild the yield yourself in about ten minutes, and the result is routinely 1 to 3 percentage points below the quoted figure once one to two months of vacancy is applied. The exercise does something more useful than winning a negotiation: it tells you whether the amenity in that particular building is paying for itself. Where the charge is high and the achieved rent reflects it, the facilities are doing their job. Where the charge is high and the achieved rent looks like a plainer building nearby, the owners are subsidising a facility list that the tenant is not paying extra for, and that gap does not close on its own.

## A worked example on the compression

Worked example, using the stress test rather than a projection. Take an apartment bought at AED 800,000 and advertised at an 8 percent gross yield. That gross implies AED 64,000 of annual rent against the purchase price.

The first correction is the capital base. Entry costs of roughly 6 to 7 percent take the committed capital to about AED 855,000, so the same rent is already a gross of about 7.5 percent rather than 8 before anything else happens.

The second correction is vacancy. At one to two months between tenancies the collected rent falls to somewhere between AED 53,000 and AED 59,000 in a turnover year, and a re-letting commission near 5 percent of annual rent applies each time the unit changes hands.

The third is the service charge, and in an amenity-led building this is the largest single deduction. Together with the landlord's other running costs it is what takes the figure into the 1 to 3 point compression the stress test predicts, landing the realistic net somewhere between 5 and 7 percent depending on the building.

None of that makes the purchase a poor one. A net in that range on a low capital base is a perfectly reasonable outcome in the Dubai market, and it is a better basis for a decision than an 8 percent figure that nobody collects. The point of the exercise is that a buyer who has done it knows what they are buying, and a buyer who has not will meet the same arithmetic later, in their own bank statements, with the purchase already made.

## What does the delivery pipeline do here?

It sets the rent in the short term, and in a growing part of Dubailand that effect is stronger than in a settled district.

New supply in an outer community competes directly for the same tenants, because the alternatives are not spread across a wide market. When several buildings hand over in the same period nearby, the immediate result is more choice for tenants and pressure on both rents and letting periods until the stock is absorbed. That is a timing effect rather than a structural flaw, and the important thing about it is that it is knowable in advance.

The check is the same one that applies across the outer belt. Establish what is under construction within a short radius of the target building and when it is scheduled to complete, using the RERA record or by asking the agent for the area pipeline data. A building with several completions landing next door in the same quarter faces a different first year from an identical building with nothing new nearby.

For a buyer holding through the build-out, the compensating point is that absorption eventually happens and the pressure lifts. For a buyer modelling an exit, it is worth knowing that a resale competes against new product with a payment plan attached for as long as the developer is still selling, which is an argument for planning the hold around the delivery cycle rather than against it.

## What should a buyer check in Majan?

Seven items, weighted toward the numbers rather than the fabric.

- The service charge per square foot for the specific building, in writing from the owners association
- The reserve fund position, since amenity-heavy buildings have more equipment to replace
- Achieved rents on comparable units in the building over the last twelve months, not asking rents
- The vacancy assumption, set at one to two months rather than at zero
- The delivery pipeline nearby, since new supply in an outer community competes directly for the same tenants
- The commute the location actually imposes, tested at peak
- The unit's size and specification against the comparable set used to price it

We verify the charge and the achieved rents before anything else, because those two numbers decide whether the advertised return survives contact with the asset.

## Who rents in Majan?

Families are the core, and the amenity is a genuine part of why they are here rather than a marketing overlay.

A family choosing an amenity-led community in the affordable band is buying facilities they could not access at the same rent in a plainer building, and children's facilities in particular are a real draw for households with young children. That produces a tenant who values what the service charge pays for, which is the healthiest version of this arrangement: the owner funds the amenity and the tenant pays for it in rent.

Budget-led households form the second group, drawn by the price point in an outer location. This group is less attached to the facilities and more sensitive to the rent, and it behaves like the mid-market apartment tenant across Dubai, on a cycle closer to the emirate's 14 to 20 month apartment average.

The mix matters for the vacancy assumption. A larger, family-configured unit in a building whose amenity is well maintained can be modelled on the longer cycle. A studio or one-bedroom competing on price alone should be modelled on the shorter one, with the void and the re-letting commission applied each time.

## Who should look at Majan, and who should not?

It suits an investor who will underwrite the net and is comfortable with a return 1 to 3 points below the advertised gross, because that is the honest number and it can still be a good one. It suits a family buying to live in who wants amenity access at an affordable entry price and accepts the outer-community commute. It suits a first Dubai purchase where the low entry price keeps the capital at risk small.

It is a poorer fit for a buyer whose case depends on the advertised gross figure holding, since it will not. It is a poorer fit for anyone unwilling to obtain the service charge and achieved rents before committing, because in an amenity-led community those two numbers are the investment case. And it is a poorer fit for a buyer who needs proximity to the coastal employment centres, which this part of Dubailand does not offer.

## Where should a buyer start?

Field note on sequence.

1. Get the service charge per square foot and the reserve fund position in writing from the owners association
2. Get achieved rents on comparable units in the same building for the last twelve months
3. Rebuild the yield yourself from those inputs, applying one to two months of vacancy
4. Compare the charge per square foot against the rent per square foot to see whether the amenity is paying for itself
5. Check the delivery pipeline nearby, since competing new supply moves rents in an outer community
6. Model the net against capital committed at transfer, meaning price plus the 6% to 8% of entry costs
