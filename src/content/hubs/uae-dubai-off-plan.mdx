## How does the Dubai off-plan structure work?

Four instruments do the work in Dubai off-plan, and three of them protect the money while none protects the calendar. They are the project escrow account required under RERA Law No. 8 of 2007, Oqood registration at 4% of price, the sale and purchase agreement, and the regulator's power to extend or cancel.

The escrow account holds buyer payments for a registered project. The developer draws against it as construction milestones are verified, rather than receiving the money directly. This is the protection that matters most, and it is the reason a payment made outside the escrow account carries a materially different risk profile from one made into it.

Oqood is the Land Department register of off-plan contracts. Registration records the buyer's interest against the unit before a title deed exists. It costs 4% of price at booking and replaces the transfer fee that a ready purchase pays at completion.

The sale and purchase agreement sets the committed handover date, the grace period, the remedy where the date is missed and the assignment threshold. It is the document that decides what happens when things go wrong, and it is the one most buyers read least carefully.

The regulator can extend a project timeline or cancel a project and order refunds. Cancellation is rare and slow. Extension is common and is the mechanism by which a slipped date becomes a legitimate date.

| Instrument | What it protects | What it does not protect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Project escrow account | Buyer funds against developer misuse | Delivery date, finish quality |
| Oqood registration | The buyer's recorded interest in the unit | Resale price at handover |
| Sale and purchase agreement | Whatever it expressly commits to | Anything it leaves to the developer's discretion |
| Regulator oversight | Project viability, refund on cancellation | Individual buyer timelines |

## What do the payment plans actually cost?

The plan shape changes the cash profile rather than the price, and comparing two plans on headline percentages is the most common modelling error in off-plan. A 60/40 structure asks 60% across construction and 40% at handover; a post-handover plan defers 40% over two to five years after keys.

| Plan shape | During construction | At handover | After handover | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 60/40 | 60% in milestone instalments | 40% balance | Nothing | Buyers with completion cash ready |
| 50/50 | 50% in instalments | 50% balance | Nothing | Balanced cash profile |
| 80/20 | 80% in instalments | 20% balance | Nothing | Buyers reducing completion exposure |
| 40/60 post-handover | 40% in instalments | 20% on keys | 40% over 2 to 5 years | Buyers using rent to service the balance |
| 1% monthly | 1% per month plus deposit | Balance on keys | Sometimes extended | Buyers prioritising low monthly outlay |

A post-handover plan is the one that most often gets modelled wrongly. The unit produces rent from handover, so the instalments can be partly serviced from income, which is the pitch. What the pitch omits is that the rent arrives net of service charges, management and vacancy, and that the instalment is fixed while the rent is not. Model the post-handover period on net rent, not gross, and assume one vacant month a year. The mechanics are worked through in [post-handover payment plans](/guides/post-handover-payment-plan-dubai/).

## How do you check a Dubai developer?

Our developer check runs to three items, in order of how much they tell you. Delivery history is the strongest signal, and a developer with 10 completed projects delivering within two quarters of commitment is a different counterparty from one with two projects and no completion record.

- Delivery history: committed against actual handover dates on the last three completed schemes
- Escrow status: the project registered and the account named in the sale and purchase agreement
- Financial depth: how many towers are selling simultaneously and whether construction depends on continued sales velocity

Delivery history is the strongest signal. A developer with ten completed projects and a documented pattern of handing over within two quarters of the committed date is a different counterparty from one with two projects and no completion record, regardless of the brochure. Ask for the completion dates of the last three projects and compare them with the dates originally committed.

Escrow status is binary and checkable. The project must be registered and the escrow account must exist. A request to pay into any other account is the single clearest signal to stop.

Financial depth is harder to assess and matters most in a slowing market. A developer selling several large towers simultaneously is dependent on continued sales velocity to fund construction on all of them. That dependency is invisible in good conditions and decisive in bad ones.

## What should be checked before signing an off-plan contract?

Read the committed date and the grace period together, because they add. A stated date of Q4 2028 with a twelve-month permitted extension is a Q4 2029 commitment, and only the second figure belongs in a model. Four further provisions carry most of the remaining risk and each is checkable before signing.

Read the assignment clause. If the plan is to sell before handover, the threshold at which assignment becomes possible and the fee charged for the no-objection certificate decide whether that plan is available at all.

Read the specification annex. Where a finish, an appliance brand or a parking allocation is described as indicative or subject to change, it will change. What is not written in the annex is not committed.

Read the remedy for delay. Most Dubai contracts give the buyer little beyond a right to terminate after an extended period, and termination returns capital without compensating for time. The realistic remedy landscape is in [developer delay compensation](/guides/dubai-developer-delay-compensation-claim/).

Finally, price the 4% Oqood registration into the entry cost rather than treating it as a completion cost. It is paid at booking, and a plan advertised as requiring 10% down actually requires 14% before the first construction instalment. The full closing cost stack, off-plan and ready, is in [the cost of buying property in Dubai](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-dubai/).

## A worked off-plan purchase, end to end

This worked example takes a one-bedroom launched at AED 1.2 million on a 60/40 plan, with handover committed for Q2 2028 and a twelve-month grace period. A plan marketed as 10% down actually requires 14% at booking once Oqood registration is added, which is AED 168,000 rather than AED 120,000.

At booking the buyer pays a 10% deposit of AED 120,000 plus Oqood registration at 4%, which is AED 48,000. That is AED 168,000 before a single construction instalment, or 14% of price, against a plan advertised as 10% down. Agency commission on a direct developer launch is often zero for the buyer, which is one of the genuine advantages of buying at launch rather than on assignment.

Across construction the buyer pays a further 50% in milestone instalments, AED 600,000. At handover the balance of 40%, AED 480,000, falls due together with the developer's handover charges and the first service charge invoice. Total outlay is about AED 1,248,000 plus service charge from the handover date.

Now apply the grace period. If handover lands in Q2 2029 rather than Q2 2028, the buyer has held AED 768,000 of capital for an extra year against no income. At a modest 5% opportunity cost that is AED 38,400, which is 3.2% of the purchase price. That figure never appears in an off-plan comparison and it is frequently larger than the discount to ready stock that justified the purchase.

The conclusion is not that off-plan is a poor product. It is that the correct comparison is the ready price today against the off-plan price plus the cost of the time, and the cost of the time has to be modelled on the grace-period date rather than the brochure date.

## Who should not buy off-plan in Dubai?

Four buyer scenarios consistently do worse in off-plan than in ready stock, and none of them is about the quality of the developer. The income-dependent buyer, the leveraged buyer at the margin, the buyer with a horizon under three years, and the buyer working to a Golden Visa deadline all face the same timing problem.

The income-dependent buyer. If the purchase is meant to produce rent within a defined window, an estimated handover date is the wrong instrument. Ready stock with an existing Ejari-registered tenancy answers that requirement directly.

The leveraged buyer at the margin. UAE banks lend against off-plan on narrower terms than against completed property, the finance costs run through the construction period against no income, and a delayed handover extends that period. Leverage magnifies the timing risk that off-plan carries.

The short-horizon buyer. A plan to exit before handover depends on the assignment threshold, on the developer's willingness to issue the no-objection certificate and on there being a resale market at the moment of exit. All three are outside the buyer's control.

The Golden Visa buyer working to a deadline. The registered value that qualifies is on the title deed, and an off-plan unit has an Oqood registration rather than a title deed until handover. Where residency has a timetable, ready stock removes the dependency entirely. The threshold mechanics are in [the UAE Golden Visa property route](/guides/uae-golden-visa-property/).

## How does the escrow account actually work?

RERA Law No. 8 of 2007 is the instrument, and the distinction it draws is the difference between an informed off-plan buyer and an optimistic one. Every registered project must hold buyer payments in a project-specific escrow account, released against milestones certified by an independent engineer rather than at the developer's discretion.

Every registered off-plan project must hold buyer payments in a project-specific escrow account at a licensed bank. The developer cannot draw from it at will. Releases are tied to verified construction progress, which means an independent engineer certifies that a milestone has been reached before the corresponding tranche is released. A percentage of the account is retained beyond completion against defects.

What that protects is the application of the money. Funds paid into escrow cannot be diverted to another project, to the developer's working capital, or to a shareholder. In a market where the historical failure mode was exactly that diversion, this is a strong protection and it is the single best reason to prefer a registered Dubai project over an unregulated off-plan market elsewhere.

What it does not protect is the schedule, the specification or the resale price. A project can be fully compliant with escrow law and still hand over two years late, deliver a smaller balcony than the brochure showed, and complete into a market where the unit is worth less than the buyer paid.

The buyer's operative rule follows directly. Every payment goes to the escrow account named in the sale and purchase agreement, referencing the unit, and never to a personal account, a corporate account or an agent. Each milestone needs its own transfer with the unit reference. Late instalments typically attract interest of about 1% per month, which is a real cost on a delayed transfer rather than a theoretical penalty.

## How should the sale and purchase agreement be read?

The sale and purchase agreement should be read clause by clause, by someone whose interests are the buyer's. Six provisions carry almost all the risk, and the two that matter most are the completion date with its grace period, which routinely adds 12 months, and the remedy on delay, which is narrower than buyers assume.

The completion date and the grace period. These are usually in different clauses and they add. A stated date of Q4 2028 with a twelve-month permitted extension is a Q4 2029 commitment, and only that second figure belongs in the model.

The remedy on delay. Read what actually happens if the extended date passes. In most Dubai contracts the answer is a right to terminate and recover payments after a further defined period, without compensation for the time. That is a narrower remedy than most buyers assume they have.

The specification annex. Anything described as indicative, representative or subject to change will change. What is committed is what is itemised. Brochure renders are not part of the contract.

The area variance clause. Most agreements permit the delivered area to differ from the contracted area by a stated percentage without adjustment, and a further band with a price adjustment. A 5% shrink on a 900 square foot apartment is 45 square feet, which is real money at AED 1,200 per square foot.

The assignment provision. It sets the threshold at which a resale before handover becomes possible, the fee for the developer no-objection certificate, and whether the developer can refuse. Without this clause read in advance, a pre-handover exit plan is an assumption rather than a plan.

The payment schedule and default terms. Milestone dates, the interest on late payment, and the point at which the developer can cancel and retain a percentage of what has been paid.

## What does assignment involve in practice?

Selling an off-plan position before handover is common, procedural and more constrained than the launch marketing implies. Most Dubai developers permit assignment once 30% to 40% of the price has been paid, subject to a no-objection certificate and an administrative fee that is sometimes a percentage rather than a flat sum.

The threshold is set by the developer, most often between 30% and 40% of the price paid. Below it the position is not transferable. Above it the seller applies for a no-objection certificate, pays an administrative fee that varies by developer and is sometimes a percentage of the price rather than a flat sum, and the transfer is registered against the new buyer.

Three things determine whether the exit is available in practice rather than on paper. The developer must be willing to issue the certificate, and some slow it deliberately when they have unsold inventory in the same project. There has to be a buyer, and the pool for an unbuilt unit is narrower than for a completed one. And the price has to work, which is where most assignment plans meet reality: the seller is asking a buyer to pay a premium over the original price for a position the developer may still be selling directly, at the original price, with a payment plan.

The honest framing is that assignment is a genuine option in a rising market and a poor one in a flat market, and nobody knows which market they will be exiting into when they sign.

## What happens at handover?

Four things fall due at handover and only the first is in the payment plan. The balance is 40% on a 60/40 structure, developer handover charges follow, the first service charge invoice typically covers a year in advance, and fit-out runs AED 45,000 to 70,000 on a one-bedroom where the strategy requires it.

The balance under the plan, which on a 60/40 structure is 40% of the price.

Developer handover charges, covering utility connection, community activation and in many schemes an initial capital contribution to the owners association reserve fund.

The first service charge invoice, typically covering a year in advance rather than in arrears. On a AED 1.2 million apartment at AED 16 per square foot that can be AED 12,000 to 16,000 payable before a tenant exists.

Fit-out, where the letting strategy requires it, at AED 45,000 to 70,000 on a one-bedroom.

The working capital requirement at handover therefore routinely runs AED 30,000 to 80,000 above the contractual balance, and a buyer who has budgeted only the plan is short at exactly the moment the unit starts costing money without earning any.

The sequence itself is a handover notice, an inspection window, a snagging list, the developer's remediation, then the handover certificate and keys. Book a professional inspection before signing the certificate. Once signed, the developer's obligation narrows to the defects liability period defined in the agreement, and anything missed becomes the owner's cost.

## What if the project is cancelled?

Project cancellation in Dubai is rare, slow and better than the alternative. The regulator can cancel a scheme that is not proceeding, at which point a liquidation process distributes the escrow balance to buyers, with recovery usually substantial but a timeline measured in years rather than months and no interest on the capital returned.

The regulator can cancel a project that is not proceeding, at which point a liquidation process distributes the escrow balance to buyers. Recovery is usually substantial but not always complete, and the timeline runs to years rather than months. Capital returned three years later without interest has still cost the buyer the use of the money.

Far more common than cancellation is extension. The regulator grants a revised timeline, the project continues, and the buyer's committed date moves. This is the normal path for a delayed project and it is why the grace period in the contract is the number that matters.

The practical protection against both is the same and it is applied before signing rather than after: a developer with completed projects, a registered escrow account, and a pricing level that does not depend on selling the whole tower before construction can proceed.

## Can you finance an off-plan purchase?

Off-plan purchases can be financed partly, and later than most buyers expect. Banks generally do not lend against an unbuilt unit on completed-property terms, so the mainstream route is that the buyer funds the payment plan and arranges a mortgage at or near handover against an asset the bank can value.

UAE banks generally do not lend against an off-plan unit during construction on the same terms as a completed property. Some offer construction-linked facilities on projects from developers they have approved, at lower loan-to-value and higher pricing. The mainstream route is that the buyer funds the payment plan from their own resources and arranges a mortgage at or near handover, against a completed asset the bank can value.

That creates a specific risk that catches leveraged buyers. The mortgage is approved at handover, against a valuation made at handover, in a market that may have moved since launch. A buyer who paid AED 1.3 million on a plan and finds the unit valued at AED 1.15 million at completion has to fund the difference in cash to complete, on top of the balance.

The conservative structure is to treat the off-plan purchase as a cash commitment and any mortgage at handover as an improvement rather than a requirement. ## Why is launch pricing set the way it is?

Launch pricing is set the way it is because the developer is buying construction funding from buyers rather than from a bank, and the 5% to 15% discount to comparable ready stock is the price of that funding. Read that way, the discount is neither a bargain nor a trick but payment for a service the buyer provides.

A launch price below comparable ready stock is not a valuation opinion. It is compensation for accepting delivery risk, forgoing income during construction, and providing the developer with staged cash at a cost below commercial finance. Read that way, the discount is neither a bargain nor a trick: it is a price for a service the buyer is providing.

Two consequences follow. First, the discount should be compared against the cost of the time rather than pocketed as a gain, which is the arithmetic worked through above. Second, a discount materially larger than the market norm signals that the developer is paying more for funding than its peers, which is usually because it can borrow less cheaply elsewhere. In a concentrated market, an unusually deep launch discount is a credit signal rather than a value opportunity.

Phase pricing works the same way. Early phases price below later ones because early buyers carry more risk, and a buyer entering at phase one is not simply getting in cheaply, they are being paid to take the delivery risk of an unproven site.

## Which off-plan communities carry which risk?

The risk in an off-plan purchase is mostly a function of where the community sits in its build-out rather than of the individual tower. A first phase on new land carries the deepest discount and the most unknowns; a final phase in a completed community behaves almost exactly like ready stock at a much smaller discount.

A first phase on virgin land carries the most delivery risk and the deepest discount. Nothing around it exists yet, the amenity case is entirely a plan, and the buyer at handover competes with phases two and three coming to market at the same time. Dubai South and the Dubailand cluster contain several examples.

A middle phase in a community that has already delivered its first handovers is the most informative position available. The developer's actual delivery performance on this specific site is visible, the first service charges are documented, and the early residents give a real signal about tenant demand. The discount is smaller than phase one and the information is far better.

A final phase in a completed community carries the least delivery risk and the least discount, and it is the position that behaves most like ready stock.

An infill tower in an established district such as Business Bay or Jumeirah Lakes Towers is a different profile again: no master plan risk because the district already exists, but a resale market at handover made up entirely of existing completed stock, which sets a hard price reference the new unit has to justify.

| Position | Discount to ready | Delivery risk | Competition at handover |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phase one, new master plan | Deepest | Highest | Developer's next phases |
| Middle phase, first handovers done | Moderate | Measurable from this site | Developer plus early resellers |
| Final phase, completed community | Smallest | Low | Existing resale stock |
| Infill tower, established district | Small | Low | Established comparable market |

The instruction that follows is unusual and worth stating plainly: the best-informed off-plan purchase is usually not the cheapest one. Paying a smaller discount for a project where the developer has already handed over on this site converts an unknown into a documented fact, and that fact is worth more than the additional percentage points.

## What does a buyer actually control?

A buyer controls very little after signing, which is why the decisions before signing carry so much weight. The developer chosen, the phase entered, the payment structure and the size of the commitment are all within the buyer's control; the handover date, the finish, the service charge and the market at completion are not.

The buyer controls the developer chosen, and delivery record is the strongest available predictor of outcome. The buyer controls the phase entered and therefore the amount of unknown accepted. The buyer controls the payment structure, and a plan weighted toward completion preserves optionality that a front-loaded plan destroys. The buyer controls whether the money is committed at a level that survives a twelve-month delay without distress.

The buyer does not control the handover date, the finish quality, the service charge that will be set, the market at completion, or whether the developer's next phase lands on the resale market at the same moment as their exit.

That asymmetry is the whole argument for treating off-plan as a long-horizon product. Every variable outside the buyer's control resolves in the buyer's favour given enough time and against them given a short one. The single test set out above, whether the committed date plus the full grace period sits before the date the money must be working, is the practical expression of the same point.

## Off-plan or ready?

Off-plan suits a long horizon, no income requirement during construction and a preference for staged payments. Ready stock suits a buyer who needs income from a known date and a documented service charge rather than a projection. Both are legitimate, and the mistake is buying one while expecting the other.

The 5% to 15% launch discount is what makes that confusion attractive, because it prices the delivery risk in a way that looks like a saving rather than like compensation for accepting an uncertain date.

One further consideration belongs in the comparison, and it is the one that decides how much of the analysis above actually applies. An off-plan unit bought to occupy is a different proposition from one bought to let, because the occupier absorbs a delay as an inconvenience while the investor absorbs it as a financing cost against no income. Occupiers can also accept a specification variance that an investor cannot, since a smaller balcony affects their comfort rather than their rent. Where the purchase is a future home and the buyer has somewhere to live in the meantime, off-plan is a reasonable way to buy a better property than the same money would buy completed. Where it is an income asset, every clause discussed above matters at full weight.

One practical rule separates the two well. Write down the date by which the money must be working, then compare it with the committed handover date plus the full grace period. If the second date is later than the first, the product is wrong regardless of how good the launch pricing looks. That single test resolves most off-plan decisions before any project comparison is needed, and it is the test that the discount to ready stock is designed to distract from.
