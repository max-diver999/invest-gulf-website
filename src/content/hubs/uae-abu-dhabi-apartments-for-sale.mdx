## Why does Al Reem Island dominate the shortlist?

Because it is the only zone in Abu Dhabi that combines scale, liquidity and a yield above 7%. Saadiyat sells faster but yields half as much. Al Reef yields more but takes three times as long to sell. Al Reem sits in the middle of both distributions, which is why it carries the deepest foreign-buyer demand in the emirate.

Inside Al Reem the building tier decides the return more than the postcode does.

| Building tier | Price per sq ft | Gross yield | Service charge per sq ft |

| Mid-range | AED 950 to 1,200 | 7% to 8% | AED 12 to 16 |
| Premium, Gate District | AED 1,200 and above | 6% to 7% | AED 16 to 22 |

The pattern is the same one that governs Dubai: amenity load raises the service charge faster than it raises the rent, so the highest net yields sit in the plainest buildings. A buyer optimising for income should be looking at budget and mid-range towers with a stable charge history rather than at the towers with the best lobby. ## What does the purchase cost?

The Abu Dhabi cost stack is roughly half the Dubai one. On an AED 1.2 million two-bedroom on Al Reem, budget about AED 50,000: a 2% transfer fee of AED 24,000, agency commission of AED 24,000, and registration administration of AED 1,000 to 4,000.

With a mortgage add 0.25% registration on the loan, about 1% bank arrangement and a valuation. The all-in cash figure of 3% to 4% compares with 6% to 7% in Dubai, and on a two-property portfolio that difference funds most of a year of service charges.

With a mortgage add 0.25% mortgage registration on the loan, a bank arrangement fee of about 1% and a valuation. The all-in cash figure of 3% to 4% compares with 6% to 7% in Dubai, and on a two-property portfolio that difference funds most of a year of service charges.

The one line that is not cheaper is time. Registration through the Department of Municipalities and Transport and, for off-plan contracts, through Tawtheeq, runs on its own timetable, and non-resident buyers should assume a longer window between agreement and title than a Dubai transaction of the same size.

## How does the tenant base differ from Dubai?

Abu Dhabi demand is employment-anchored rather than tourism-anchored. Tenants come from government, the energy sector, the ADGM financial cluster, the universities and the cultural institutions on Saadiyat, which produces three effects a Dubai model does not anticipate and which move vacancy assumptions into the 4% to 8% band.

Tenancies run longer. An Abu Dhabi professional tenant who has taken a role in the capital is not moving for a shorter commute in eighteen months, and the practical result is lower vacancy and fewer re-letting commissions across a five-year hold. Vacancy assumptions of 4% to 8% by community are realistic here, at the lower end for Saadiyat and Yas.

Seasonality is weaker. Dubai rents flex with a tourism cycle and a short-let market that competes for the same stock. Abu Dhabi has far less of both, so rents move more slowly in both directions.

Short-let is a marginal strategy rather than a core one. Yas Island supports genuine leisure-driven short-stay demand around the entertainment assets, and Saadiyat supports some cultural tourism. Elsewhere the numbers do not work, and a purchase justified by short-let projections outside those two islands deserves scepticism.

## What should an Abu Dhabi apartment buyer check?

Our pre-offer checklist runs to four items here, and the first has no analogue in Dubai. Confirm investment zone status in the Department of Municipalities and Transport record rather than in the listing, because with nine zones against more than 60 in Dubai this is the check that voids a purchase rather than repricing it. The zone list is in [Abu Dhabi freehold areas](/guides/abu-dhabi-freehold-areas/).

Read the service charge trajectory across two years of approved budgets. A tower moving from AED 12 to AED 16 per square foot has moved net yield by most of a percentage point, and it will keep moving.

Check the tower's chiller arrangement. District cooling billed on a separate meter with a fixed capacity charge is common in Abu Dhabi towers, is payable whether or not the unit is occupied, and does not appear in the service charge statement.

Establish the realistic exit window before buying at the yield end. Al Reef at 9.5% gross is an excellent income asset and a poor short-hold one, and the days-on-market figures make that explicit rather than implied.

## Studio, one-bedroom or two-bedroom in Abu Dhabi?

The unit-mix decision works differently here than in Dubai because the tenant base is different. Abu Dhabi demand skews toward tenants with families or a housing allowance covering a one-bedroom, which makes studios a thinner market than in Jumeirah Village Circle and one-bedrooms the most liquid unit type in the emirate.

Two-bedrooms benefit most from the emirate's longer tenancies and 4% to 8% vacancy assumptions, because a family on a two-year lease near a school turns over less than any comparable Dubai unit.

Studios are a thinner market. Abu Dhabi's employment-anchored demand skews toward tenants with families or with a housing allowance that covers a one-bedroom, and the studio segment consequently lets more slowly and resells to a narrower pool. Where Dubai studios in JVC are a mainstream investment product, Abu Dhabi studios are a niche one.

One-bedrooms are the volume product on Al Reem and the most liquid unit type open to foreign buyers in the emirate. They let to the single largest tenant segment, resell to both investors and end-users, and carry the best combination of yield and exit speed anywhere in the nine zones.

Two-bedrooms are the family product and the one that benefits most from Abu Dhabi's longer tenancies. A two-bedroom on Al Reem or Al Raha Beach let to a family on a two-year lease with a school nearby will turn over less than any comparable Dubai unit, and across a five-year hold that saves two vacant months and two re-letting commissions. The gross yield is lower than a one-bedroom in the same tower; the net difference is much smaller than the gross gap suggests.

Three-bedroom apartments are scarce and behave like villas: slow to let, slow to sell, and priced for end-users rather than investors. Unless the buyer intends to occupy, the yield case rarely supports them.

## Is short-let worth it in Abu Dhabi?

Short-let is worth it on Yas Island and parts of Saadiyat, and almost nowhere else in the emirate. Yas carries genuine leisure demand around the entertainment assets and the racing calendar, Saadiyat carries cultural tourism, and both markets are small relative to Dubai's with a less developed operator ecosystem.

Yas has genuine leisure demand around the entertainment assets and the racing calendar, which produces real nightly rates for part of the year and thin occupancy for the rest. Saadiyat carries cultural tourism with a similar seasonal shape. Both markets are small relative to Dubai's, the operator ecosystem is less developed, and the permit framework is administered by the Department of Culture and Tourism rather than by the Dubai equivalent.

The arithmetic that decides it is the same everywhere: furnishing at AED 45,000 to 70,000 on a one-bedroom, an operator taking 15% to 20% of gross, utilities moving to the owner, and a permit fee per unit. Against Abu Dhabi's longer standard tenancies and lower vacancy, the incremental net gain outside Yas and Saadiyat is usually negative rather than small. Model long-let and treat any short-let income as optional upside.

- Parking allocation, tiered in newer towers and thin in the earliest ones
- Plant age, since Reem's first towers are now past a decade
- Precinct, because the Gate District, Shams and Najmat carry different unit mixes

## How does the Abu Dhabi tenant market behave?

The Abu Dhabi tenant market behaves differently enough from Dubai that a Dubai model overstates turnover cost and understates letting time. Tenants move less because year one costs them roughly 8% to 10% of annual rent in fees and deposits, and the letting cycle is slower because demand arrives on employment timetables.

Abu Dhabi tenants move less. Year one for a tenant here carries an agency fee of about 5% of annual rent, one to two months of security deposit, Tawtheeq-related components of roughly 3% to 5% of annual rent, and an unfurnished fit-out of AED 25,000 to 60,000 on a three-bedroom. That front-loaded cost is a strong disincentive to move, which is why vacancy assumptions of 4% to 8% are realistic and why the lower end applies to family-suitable units near schools.

Against that, the letting cycle is slower. The tenant pool is employment-anchored and arrives on employment timetables rather than continuously, so a unit that falls vacant outside the main hiring and school-year windows can sit longer than an equivalent Dubai unit would.

The practical consequence for a landlord is to weight tenant retention above rent maximisation. An increase that prompts a good tenant to move costs a vacant period plus a re-letting commission in a market where re-letting is slower, and that arithmetic usually loses to the increase.

Contracts register through Tawtheeq, which is what makes a lease enforceable and a rent verifiable. The rent regulation framework differs from Dubai's banded RERA calculator, so confirm the current position rather than assuming the Dubai rules transfer.

## What does financing an Abu Dhabi apartment involve?

Financing an Abu Dhabi apartment involves the federal ceilings and one local complication. Banks lend up to 80% to a UAE national and 75% to an expatriate resident on a first property under AED 5 million, with non-residents at 50% to 65%, and the complication is that valuations here land below agreed prices more often.

The central bank sets loan-to-value limits across the UAE: up to 80% for a UAE national and 75% for an expatriate resident on a first property under AED 5 million, stepping down above that value and for subsequent properties, with non-residents typically at 50% to 65% and at pricing above resident rates. Those are in [UAE central bank mortgage rules](/guides/uae-central-bank-mortgage-rules/), and the national lenders are the same ones listed in [the UAE mortgage bank list](/guides/uae-mortgage-banks-list/).

The local complication is valuation. Abu Dhabi has far fewer registered comparable transactions than Dubai, and a valuer facing thin evidence prices conservatively. Valuation shortfalls against agreed prices are more common here, and the buyer funds the difference in cash on top of the deposit and the fees.

Two responses follow. Get an indicative valuation before agreeing a price on any leveraged purchase. And build the possibility of a shortfall into the cash plan, because on a AED 1.4 million purchase a 6% shortfall is AED 84,000 that has to appear at completion.

The fee side is lighter than Dubai. Mortgage registration is 0.25% of the loan and the bank arrangement fee about 1%, the same as Dubai, but the 2% transfer fee against 4% means the total leveraged entry cost still lands well below the Dubai equivalent.

## A worked Al Reem purchase

This worked example takes a two-bedroom on Al Reem Island at AED 1.2 million, 950 square feet, in a mid-range tower with a service charge of AED 14 per square foot, bought for cash and let long-term. The purpose is to show how far the net figure sits below the 7.5% gross a listing would quote.

Acquisition. The 2% transfer fee is AED 24,000. Agency commission at 2% is AED 24,000. Registration administration is about AED 2,500. Closing costs total AED 50,500, an effective premium of 4.2%, taking the all-in figure to AED 1,250,500. The same purchase price in Dubai would carry roughly AED 78,000 of costs.

Income. A mid-range Reem two-bedroom lets in the AED 90,000 range, which is 7.5% gross on the purchase price and 7.2% on the all-in figure.

Deductions. The service charge is AED 13,300. Management at 6% of rent is AED 5,400. A vacancy allowance at 7% is AED 6,300. A maintenance provision at 0.5% of value is AED 6,000. Tawtheeq and utility reconnection between tenancies add roughly AED 1,500 in an average year. Total deductions are AED 32,500.

Net rent is about AED 57,500, which is 4.8% on the purchase price and 4.6% on the all-in figure. The gap between gross and net is 2.7 percentage points.

Run the same exercise on a budget tower at AED 950,000 with a AED 11 charge and a rent of AED 78,000. Gross is 8.2%. Deductions total about AED 26,000, so net rent is AED 52,000, or 5.5% on the purchase price. The cheaper tower nets 0.7 percentage points more on 21% less capital, which is the clearest illustration available that in this market the building tier matters more than the island.

Break-even on the entry cost, from income alone, is roughly eleven months on the budget tower and thirteen on the mid-range one. Both are shorter than the Dubai equivalent, and that is entirely the transfer fee difference.

## How do the value communities differ from the island market?

Al Reef and Al Ghadeer are frequently grouped together as the yield end at 8% to 9.5% gross, and they answer to entirely different demand. Al Reef serves airport, logistics and services employment inside Abu Dhabi; Al Ghadeer sits on the Dubai border and serves cross-emirate commuters.

Al Reef sits near the airport and serves aviation, logistics and services employment. Its stock is a mix of apartments and villas built to a straightforward specification, its charges are the lowest in the emirate, and its tenant base is domestic rather than international. That is why it produces 9% to 9.5% gross and why its resale buyer pool is largely UAE residents rather than overseas investors, which is the direct cause of the 90 to 150 day sale period.

Al Ghadeer sits on the Abu Dhabi side of the Dubai border and serves a different tenant entirely: households working in Dubai who want Abu Dhabi pricing, and households working in Abu Dhabi who want a shorter commute to Dubai on weekends. Its demand is therefore partly a function of the Dubai rental market rather than the Abu Dhabi one, which makes it the only Abu Dhabi community with a genuine cross-emirate hedge. It runs 8% to 8.5% gross at AED 600 to 900 per square foot, the lowest entry pricing in the country.

The practical distinction for a buyer is what each one is exposed to. Al Reef's yield depends on Abu Dhabi employment continuing at the airport and in services. Al Ghadeer's depends on the Dubai rent level staying high enough to push commuters across the border. Neither is a risk in the ordinary course, and they are different risks, which is a reason to prefer one over the other rather than to treat them as interchangeable.

Masdar City deserves a mention alongside them for the same reason. Its 6% to 7.5% gross comes with a tenant base tied almost entirely to the sustainability cluster and the university campus, which is the narrowest demand base of any zone open to foreign buyers. Stable while the anchor holds, thin if it does not.

## What does the first year of ownership actually cost?

The first year costs more than the purchase model shows, and that gap separates a model from a plan. The service charge is payable from the transfer date whether or not the unit is let, typically a year in advance, which on a 950 square foot two-bedroom at AED 14 is AED 13,300 before a tenant exists.

The service charge is payable from the transfer date whether or not the unit is let, in advance rather than in arrears in most Abu Dhabi towers. On a 950 square foot two-bedroom at AED 14 that is AED 13,300 due before a tenant exists.

The district cooling capacity charge, where applicable, starts on the same date and runs through any vacant period.

Letting the unit takes time. In a market where tenants arrive on employment timetables, a unit completing outside the main hiring windows can wait, and the first tenancy is the slowest one because there is no incumbent to renew.

The letting agency fee on a new tenancy is typically 5% of annual rent, charged once.

Furnishing, where the strategy calls for it, runs AED 45,000 to 70,000 on a one-bedroom and AED 65,000 to 100,000 on a two-bedroom.

A realistic first-year cash requirement above the purchase price and closing costs is therefore AED 20,000 to 30,000 unfurnished, and considerably more furnished, against an income stream that may not start for two or three months. Models that show income from the completion date are the single most common reason a first Abu Dhabi purchase feels worse than it performs.

## What should an apartment buyer avoid?

Five mistakes account for most of the disappointment in a first Abu Dhabi purchase, and the first is buying the amenity. Towers with heavy leisure provision carry service charges of AED 16 to 22 per square foot that the rent does not fully recover, and the net yield gap runs consistently in the plain tower's favour.

Assuming short-let works. Outside Yas and parts of Saadiyat, Abu Dhabi does not have the leisure demand to support it, the operator ecosystem is thinner than Dubai's, and the permit sits with the Department of Culture and Tourism. A purchase justified on short-let projections elsewhere in the emirate deserves scepticism.

Buying yield without checking the exit. Al Reef at 9% to 9.5% gross takes 90 to 150 days to sell with a 5% to 12% negotiation band. That is a strong income asset and a poor short-hold one, and the yield is the compensation rather than a free gain.

Trusting a listing on zone status. Freehold is limited to the nine investment zones and, inside Khalifa City, to specific projects. The Department of Municipalities and Transport record is the authority. This is the one check in Abu Dhabi that voids a purchase rather than merely repricing it, and it is in [Abu Dhabi freehold areas](/guides/abu-dhabi-freehold-areas/).

Ignoring the capacity charge. District cooling is charged on availability as well as consumption, which means a vacant month still costs money, and it is invisible in every yield calculation that starts from the service charge statement.

## How does an Abu Dhabi apartment exit?

An Abu Dhabi apartment exits slower than a Dubai one and predictably so, which makes it plannable. Saadiyat clears in 30 to 60 days with almost no negotiation, Yas in 45 to 75, Al Reem in 60 to 90, Al Ghadeer in 75 to 120 and Al Reef in 90 to 150 days with a band reaching 12%.

Saadiyat sells in 30 to 60 days on average with almost no negotiation, Yas in 45 to 75 with 0% to 5%, Al Reem in 60 to 90 with 2% to 8%, Al Ghadeer in 75 to 120 with 3% to 8% and Al Reef in 90 to 150 with 5% to 12%.

Three factors move a specific unit within its community band. A tenanted unit sells more slowly to owner-occupiers, because the tenancy runs with the property and the buyer cannot take possession before it ends. A unit in a building with a rising service charge sells at a discount, because every buyer modelling net yield capitalises the charge. And a unit priced from a listing rather than from registered comparables sits, because the gap between asking and transacted prices is wider here than in Dubai.

The seller's practical checklist is short. Request the mortgage liability letter on day one if there is a loan. Clear service charges to date, because the developer no-objection certificate depends on it. Confirm the tenancy position before promising vacant possession. And if the property supports a Golden Visa, arrange the replacement asset before listing.
