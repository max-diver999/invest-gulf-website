The UAE is seven property markets under one federal visa rule. The rule is the same everywhere: AED 2 million of registered value buys a ten-year residency, no income tax applies, and foreign ownership is permitted in designated areas. Everything else differs by emirate, starting with how many designated areas there are, which ranges from more than sixty in Dubai to none at all in Sharjah.

## Dubai or Abu Dhabi?

Dubai against Abu Dhabi is the real decision for most foreign buyers, because the northern emirates serve a different budget entirely. Dubai offers more than 60 freehold zones and over 205,000 registered transactions a year; Abu Dhabi offers nine investment zones, prices roughly 30% lower and a 2% transfer fee against 4%.

Dubai wins on choice and liquidity, which supports valuation on a mortgage, a fast exit and short-let as a genuine strategy. Abu Dhabi wins on cost, with an all-in closing stack of 3% to 4% against 6% to 7% and value-community yields that exceed anything Dubai offers.

Dubai wins on choice and liquidity. More than sixty freehold zones, product from AED 480,000 studios to eight-figure Palm villas, and more than 205,000 registered transactions a year producing comparables for almost any unit. That depth supports valuation on a mortgage, supports a fast exit and supports short-let as a genuine strategy rather than a marginal one.

Abu Dhabi wins on cost. Equivalent product prices roughly 30% below Dubai per square foot, the transfer fee is half, and the all-in closing stack is 3% to 4% against 6% to 7%. Yields at the value end, Al Reef and Al Ghadeer, exceed anything Dubai offers. The trade is a nine-zone universe and a resale market where a high-yield unit can take five months to sell.

The decision rule that holds up: if the buyer may need to exit on a timetable, or wants a wide community choice, Dubai's premium is worth paying. If the buyer will hold for income across a long period and the required product exists inside the nine zones, Abu Dhabi is the better arithmetic. The two markets are covered in detail on [property for sale in Dubai](/uae/dubai/) and [property for sale in Abu Dhabi](/uae/abu-dhabi/).

## What do the northern emirates offer?

The northern emirates offer the lowest entry prices in the country and the thinnest markets, which is a single trade rather than two separate facts. Ras Al Khaimah is the most developed at AED 700 to 1,600 per square foot with yields of 6% to 9%, and Ajman offers the lowest apartment prices in the UAE.

- Ras Al Khaimah: freehold on Al Marjan Island, Al Hamra Village and Mina Al Arab, leisure-led thesis
- Ajman: freehold in selected communities, yields of 7% to 9%, buyer pool largely UAE residents
- Sharjah: usufruct to 100 years rather than freehold, driven by Dubai commuter demand
- Umm Al Quwain and Fujairah: markets too thin for reliable yield figures

Ras Al Khaimah is the most developed of the four, with freehold communities on Al Marjan Island, Al Hamra Village and Mina Al Arab, and a leisure-led investment thesis anchored on the integrated resort under construction on Al Marjan. Prices run AED 700 to 1,600 per square foot with yields of 6% to 9%, and the buyer pool has broadened since the resort was announced. The emirate is covered in [the Ras Al Khaimah property investment guide](/guides/ras-al-khaimah-property-investment-guide/).

Ajman offers the lowest apartment entry prices in the UAE, with freehold available in selected communities and yields in the 7% to 9% band. The constraint is exit: the buyer pool is largely UAE residents rather than international, and days-on-market figures are longer than anywhere in Dubai.

Sharjah does not offer freehold to foreign nationals. What is available is a usufruct interest of up to 100 years in designated developments, which is a real and registrable right but a different asset with a narrower resale market. Buyers attracted by Sharjah's rents, driven by Dubai commuters seeking lower housing costs, should understand that they are buying a long lease rather than a title.

Umm Al Quwain and Fujairah have small freehold offerings and markets too thin to support reliable yield figures. They suit a buyer with a specific reason to be there rather than a portfolio allocation.

## What is the same in every emirate?

Four things are identical across all seven emirates, and they are the reason the UAE is treated as one market. The AED 2 million Golden Visa threshold is federal, the tax position is federal, the central bank mortgage ceilings are federal, and the buying mechanics rhyme everywhere under different names.

- Golden Visa: AED 2 million of registered title value, in the applicant's own name
- Tax: no annual property tax, no capital gains tax on a personal sale, no income tax on rent
- Mortgages: up to 80% for nationals, 75% for expatriate residents, 50% to 65% for non-residents
- Mechanics: a land department, a registered title, an escrow requirement on off-plan

The Golden Visa threshold is federal. AED 2 million of registered value qualifies for the ten-year residency wherever the property sits, measured on the title rather than on the agreed price. The practical consequence is that the same qualifying purchase buys very different amounts of property depending on the emirate, and the mechanics are in [the UAE Golden Visa property route](/guides/uae-golden-visa-property/).

The tax position is federal. No annual property tax, no capital gains tax on a personal sale, no personal income tax on rent. The corporate tax regime introduced in 2023 does not reach personal residential rental income.

The mortgage ceilings are federal. Central bank rules set loan-to-value limits at up to 80% for nationals and 75% for expatriate residents on a first property under AED 5 million, with non-residents borrowing on narrower terms of roughly 50% to 65%. Those ceilings are in [UAE central bank mortgage rules](/guides/uae-central-bank-mortgage-rules/), and the lender list in [the UAE mortgage bank list](/guides/uae-mortgage-banks-list/).

The buying mechanics rhyme. Each emirate has a land department, a registered title, an escrow requirement on off-plan and a contract register. The names differ, the sequence does not.

## What differs enough to change a decision?

The zone rule differs most, and it is the first filter rather than a detail. More than 60 freehold zones in Dubai against nine investment zones in Abu Dhabi against no freehold at all in Sharjah removes most of the country for most buyers before any budget or yield question is asked.

The transfer fee differs by a factor of two between Dubai and Abu Dhabi, which on a AED 2 million purchase is roughly AED 40,000.

Liquidity differs by an order of magnitude. Dubai's transaction volume is what makes a fast exit possible and a mortgage valuation predictable. Nothing else in the UAE approaches it.

The tenant base differs. Dubai demand carries a large tourism and short-let component. Abu Dhabi demand is employment-anchored, which produces longer tenancies and lower seasonality. The northern emirates are driven by domestic affordability and Dubai commuting.

## Who decides what: federal against emirate

The UAE is a federation, and property rules sit at both levels. Knowing which level a rule belongs to tells you whether it changes when you cross an emirate boundary, and the pattern is consistent enough to remember: money and immigration are federal, land and tenancy are emirate. That is why the AED 2 million visa threshold and the zero income tax hold everywhere while the transfer fee moves from 2% to 4%, the registry changes name, and the rent regulation regime differs entirely.

| Rule | Level | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Golden Visa threshold, AED 2 million | Federal | Identical in every emirate, measured on registered title value |
| Personal income tax, none | Federal | No tax on rental income anywhere in the country |
| Capital gains tax on a personal sale, none | Federal | Applies country-wide |
| Mortgage loan-to-value ceilings | Federal, central bank | Same limits in every emirate |
| VAT at 5% | Federal | Applies to agency commission and some commercial property, not residential resale |
| Which areas foreigners may own | Emirate | 60-plus zones in Dubai, nine in Abu Dhabi, none freehold in Sharjah |
| Transfer or registration fee | Emirate | 4% Dubai, 2% Abu Dhabi, 2% to 4% northern emirates |
| Land registry and title | Emirate | Dubai Land Department, Abu Dhabi Department of Municipalities and Transport |
| Tenancy registration | Emirate | Ejari in Dubai, Tawtheeq in Abu Dhabi |
| Rent increase regulation | Emirate | Dubai has the banded RERA index under Decree 43/2013; other emirates differ |

The pattern is consistent: money and immigration are federal, land and tenancy are emirate. A buyer comparing emirates should therefore expect the visa and tax position to be identical and everything about ownership and letting to differ.

## How does the Golden Visa actually work?

The property route grants a ten-year renewable residency to an owner whose property carries a registered value of AED 2 million or more.

Four details decide whether a given purchase qualifies.

The figure that counts is the value registered on the title, not the agreed price and not a valuation obtained separately. Where those differ, the registered figure governs.

The property must be held in the applicant's own name. A corporate holding structure does not qualify under this category, which is the main reason most foreign buyers should hold personally.

Leverage complicates it. The qualifying value takes the bank position into account, so a mortgaged purchase at the threshold is not automatically a qualifying one. Confirm with the issuing authority before treating a leveraged purchase as a residency route.

An off-plan unit has a contract registration rather than a title until handover. Where residency is on a timetable, ready stock removes the dependency entirely.

The visa is attached to the asset, which has a consequence most owners discover late: selling the qualifying property ends the residency unless a replacement asset is registered first. Anyone selling should arrange the replacement before listing. The full mechanics are in [the UAE Golden Visa property route](/guides/uae-golden-visa-property/).

Because the threshold is federal while prices are not, the same qualifying purchase buys very different assets. In prime Dubai it reaches a studio or a small one-bedroom. On Al Reem Island it reaches a two-bedroom yielding 7% or more. In Ajman it reaches a townhouse. For a buyer whose objective is residency with a working income asset attached, that spread is the most useful fact on this page.

## What tax actually applies?

No property tax applies locally, and possibly a great deal at home, which is the part that catches new buyers. Inside the UAE there is no annual property tax, no capital gains tax on a personal sale and no income tax on rent, and VAT at 5% reaches agency commission rather than residential resale.

Inside the UAE there is no annual property tax, no capital gains tax on a personal sale and no personal income tax on rent. The corporate tax regime introduced in 2023 does not reach personal residential rental income. VAT at 5% applies to agency commission and to some commercial property, not to residential resale. The only transaction tax is the emirate transfer fee, paid once.

Outside the UAE the position depends entirely on the buyer's tax residence. A UK tax resident is taxed on worldwide income and reports Dubai rent in the United Kingdom from the first year, following the 2025 changes to the non-domicile regime. An Indian resident faces a reporting obligation with its own remittance framework. A US citizen is taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live. Buyers from most European jurisdictions face similar treatment.

The practical instruction is to establish the home-country position before purchase rather than after the first rent arrives, because the structure of the purchase is easier to arrange at the start than to unwind later. ## How does money move into a UAE purchase?

Moving money into a UAE purchase stalls more transactions than any other step, and the requirements are the same in every emirate. Funds must arrive from an account in the buyer's own name with documented source of funds, and on off-plan every instalment goes to the named escrow account referencing the unit.

Funds must arrive from an account in the buyer's own name, with documentation showing where the money came from. A transfer routed through a third party, an exchange house or a corporate account the buyer does not obviously control triggers questions that take weeks to answer.

For an off-plan purchase, every instalment goes to the project escrow account named in the sale and purchase agreement, referencing the unit, and never anywhere else. Late instalments typically attract interest of about 1% per month.

For a resale, funds are handed over at the registration office in the form the registry requires, which in Dubai means manager cheques at the trustee appointment.

Three preparatory steps save weeks. Open the receiving arrangements before agreeing a completion date rather than after. Assemble the source-of-funds documentation, meaning payslips, sale contracts, dividend statements or whatever explains the money, in advance. And where the purchase is remote, start the power of attorney chain early: notarised in the country of signing, legalised or attested as required, translated into Arabic, which takes seven to fourteen days. The mechanics are in [buying UAE property by bank transfer](/guides/buying-property-uae-bank-transfer/).

## What happens to a UAE property on death?

Without a registered will, a non-Muslim owner's UAE estate can be distributed in a way that does not match home-country expectations, and the process is slow. Registered estates typically clear probate in 4 to 12 weeks through the DIFC Courts, against 12 to 36 months where nothing has been registered.

The DIFC Wills Service Centre allows non-Muslim individuals to register a will under common law principles, with probate through the DIFC Courts. Registered estates typically clear probate in four to twelve weeks against twelve to thirty-six months for unregistered ones. A property will covering only Dubai-registered property is the narrower and cheaper option; a full will covers wider UAE assets. Abu Dhabi has an equivalent registry through ADGM covering Abu Dhabi assets.

For an owner whose UAE property is a meaningful part of the estate, registering a will is the highest-value administrative step available, it costs a fraction of the property's transaction costs, and it is routinely the last thing anyone does. The comparison of the two registries is in [UAE wills, DIFC and ADGM](/guides/uae-will-difc-adgm/).

## How does a purchase run, end to end?

The purchase sequence is the same across the country and only the registry and the timing change. A cash resale in Dubai clears in three to six weeks from agreed price to title, Abu Dhabi and the northern emirates run longer, and a mortgage adds two to five weeks anywhere in the federation.

| Stage | Dubai | Abu Dhabi | Northern emirates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Offer and reservation | 1 to 3 days | 1 to 3 days | 1 to 3 days |
| Sale contract | Form F, same week | Standard sale agreement, same week | Developer or agency form |
| Developer no-objection | 1 to 7 business days | 3 to 10 business days | 3 to 10 business days |
| Mortgage steps if financed | 2 to 4 weeks | 3 to 5 weeks | 3 to 5 weeks |
| Registration | Trustee office, 2 to 5 days | Department of Municipalities and Transport, 1 to 2 weeks | Emirate land department |
| Title issued | Same day at trustee | With registration | With registration |

A cash resale in Dubai therefore clears in three to six weeks from agreed price to title. Abu Dhabi and the northern emirates run longer, and a mortgaged purchase adds two to five weeks anywhere.

The bottleneck is almost never the buyer. It is the developer certificate on a building with a service charge arrear, or the seller's bank on a mortgaged property, where discharge runs on the lender's timetable rather than the transaction's. Asking on day one whether the seller has a mortgage changes the realistic completion date by a month, and it is the single most useful question in the process. ## What does a buyer need to produce?

Buyers need less documentation than in most jurisdictions and more than the marketing suggests, and the list is the same in every emirate. A passport is the identity requirement, no residence visa is needed to buy, and the substantive test on a cash purchase is documented source of funds.

A passport. No residence visa is required to buy anywhere in the UAE, and no physical presence is required where a power of attorney is in place.

Documented source of funds, from an account in the buyer's own name. This is the substantive requirement on a cash purchase and the most common cause of delay.

For a mortgage, six months of bank statements, proof of income, and for the self-employed two to three years of company accounts. Non-resident applications take longer and are declined more often on documentation than on affordability.

For a remote purchase, a power of attorney notarised in the country of signing, legalised or apostilled, attested where required and translated into Arabic by a legal translator. Seven to fourteen days, started before agreeing a completion date rather than after.

## Personal name or company?

Personal ownership suits most foreign buyers in every emirate, for three reasons that hold across the country. It is the only route qualifying for the AED 2 million Golden Visa threshold, it carries no entity maintenance cost, and it transfers as a routine registry transaction rather than as a share sale.

It is the only route that qualifies for the Golden Visa under the real-estate category, because the threshold is measured against a title in the applicant's own name.

It is cheaper, with no entity to establish or maintain.

It is simpler at resale, because a property transfer is a routine registry transaction while a share transfer in a property-holding company is not.

Corporate holding has real uses: multiple owners with differing shares are cleaner as shareholdings, a portfolio assembled for sale as a whole transfers more cheaply as shares, and some estate-planning objectives are easier at entity level. The constraint is that the land registry must recognise the entity type, which narrows the options considerably and differs by emirate. Confirm the specific structure with the relevant registry before committing, because a structure that cannot be registered on title is not a structure at all.

## Which emirate suits which buyer, in practice?

Four buyer scenarios recur often enough to be worth naming, and each points at a different emirate for a reason that is not the yield. The buyer who will never visit, the buyer relocating for work, the buyer optimising the AED 2 million residency threshold, and the buyer wanting the lowest entry price.

The buyer who will never visit. Dubai, and specifically ready stock in a community with deep comparables. Everything about a remote purchase is easier where the market is liquid, the valuation is uncontroversial and the management ecosystem is mature.

The buyer relocating for work. The emirate of employment, without exception. Commuting between Dubai and Abu Dhabi is an hour and a half each way on a bad day, and households that do it consistently underestimate what it costs them over several years.

The buyer optimising the residency threshold. Abu Dhabi, where AED 2 million buys roughly 30% more property than in Dubai and the qualifying title works identically. Al Reem Island is the specific answer more often than any other zone in the country.

The buyer wanting the lowest entry price. Ajman or Al Ghadeer on the Abu Dhabi side of the Dubai border, both under AED 900 per square foot, both with yields in the 7% to 9% band, and both with exits measured in months rather than weeks. That trade is acceptable on a long hold and painful on a short one.

## What does the whole country get wrong about itself?

Two misconceptions recur about this market, and correcting them improves most purchase decisions. The first is treating the UAE as one market because the visa and tax rules are federal. The second is reading yield without reading exit, where the 9%-plus communities take a quarter or more to sell.

The first is treating the UAE as one market because the visa and tax rules are federal. They are, and nothing else is. Choice of emirate changes the zone rule, the transfer fee, the registry, the tenancy regime, the rent regulation, the liquidity and the tenant base. Those differences are larger than the differences between communities inside a single emirate.

The second is reading yield without reading exit. The highest yields in the country sit in Al Reef, Al Ghadeer and Ajman, and all three take a quarter or more to sell with negotiation bands reaching 12%. The lowest yields sit in Saadiyat and prime Dubai, both of which clear in weeks. That relationship is the market pricing liquidity, it is stable, and it means the holding period rather than the yield number should decide the shortlist.

A buyer who holds both corrections in mind will make a better decision here than one who has read every price table in the country.

What the table does not capture is the cost of the exit. A Dubai unit that sells in six weeks and an Abu Dhabi unit that sells in five months carry different holding costs during the sale, and on a leveraged position that difference can exceed the transfer fee saving entirely.

## An insider tip on choosing between emirates

Insider tip: run the same budget through both markets in full before choosing, because the net yields land closer together than either market's marketing suggests. AED 1.3 million buys a two-bedroom on Al Reem Island in Abu Dhabi at roughly 4.6% net, or a one-bedroom in Jumeirah Village Circle in Dubai at roughly 4.7% net, a difference inside the margin of error on both. What actually differs is everything around the number: Abu Dhabi delivers a second bedroom and saves about AED 31,000 at entry through a 2% transfer fee against 4%, while Dubai delivers a resale market that absorbs the unit in weeks rather than months. The decision is therefore about holding period and exit optionality rather than about yield, and framing it as a yield comparison produces the wrong answer.
