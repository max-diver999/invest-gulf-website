## What Is Al Etihad Credit Bureau?

Al Etihad Credit Bureau is a UAE federal government entity that operates under the oversight of the UAE Central Bank. Established under Federal Law No. 6 of 2010 and formally operational since 2014, AECB collects credit data from all licensed financial institutions in the UAE, including banks, finance companies, and telecoms providers, and compiles it into individual credit reports and a single numerical score.

AECB is not a bank or a lender. It is an information intermediary: it receives data from lenders and provides that data back to lenders (with your consent) when you apply for credit. AECB also sells reports directly to consumers via its app and website. The bureau covers UAE nationals and expatriate residents alike. As of 2025, AECB holds data on over 7 million individuals and more than 700,000 businesses.

Every major UAE bank, Emirates NBD, ADCB, FAB, HSBC UAE, Mashreq, CBD, and others, is a mandatory data contributor. This means your entire UAE credit history, across all institutions, is consolidated into a single report. There is no hiding a missed credit card payment with one bank when you apply for a mortgage at another.

## How the UAE Credit Score Works

Your AECB score is a three-digit number ranging from 300 to 900. Higher scores indicate lower credit risk. The score is calculated using a proprietary algorithm that weighs several categories of information from your credit report. While AECB does not publish its exact formula, the principal factors are consistent with global credit scoring models.

**Payment history** is the most heavily weighted factor, typically accounting for roughly 35% of your score. A single missed payment that goes 30 days past due can reduce a good score by 50–100 points. Payments that reach 90 days past due cause substantially more damage and trigger formal default classifications on your report.

**Credit utilisation** measures how much of your available revolving credit you are using at any point in time. Using above 30% of your total credit card limits consistently signals financial stress to scoring models. The optimal range is below 30%; the sweet spot that most scoring professionals target is below 20%.

**Length of credit history** rewards older, well-managed accounts. A UAE expat who opened their first credit card six months ago will score lower on this dimension than someone who has maintained a clean 5-year credit card account, even if both have perfect payment records.

**Credit mix**, having a combination of instalment debt (car loan, personal loan) and revolving credit (credit card), modestly improves scores compared to holding only one type of credit product.

**Recent applications** penalise borrowers who have applied for multiple credit products in a short timeframe. Each hard inquiry from a lender typically reduces the score by 5–10 points and remains visible on the report for 12 months. Applying to five banks for mortgages simultaneously is visible to all five underwriters and signals financial distress.

## How to Check Your AECB Credit Score

Your AECB credit score and full report are available through three channels. The AECB mobile app (available on iOS and Android) is the fastest and cheapest route for most people. You can also access reports at aecb.gov.ae, or visit selected government service centres and typing centres across the UAE.

A basic score check, showing your score number and a brief summary, costs AED 10.50. A comprehensive credit report with full tradeline detail, including every account, payment history, and inquiry on record, costs AED 84. Both are available instantly online.

You are entitled to check your own score as many times as you wish. Self-checks are soft inquiries and have zero impact on your score. This differs from how banks check your score: when a lender runs a formal credit check as part of a loan application, that generates a hard inquiry.

Before applying for a UAE mortgage, check your AECB report at least 90 days in advance. This gives you time to identify any errors, dispute inaccuracies, and address any issues that could affect your application. Errors on credit reports, wrong balances, settled debts still showing as open, accounts that belong to someone else, are not uncommon and can be disputed directly with AECB.

## How Your Credit Score Affects UAE Mortgage Eligibility

Every UAE mortgage lender checks your AECB score as part of underwriting. For guidance on current market rates and which banks are active in the mortgage market, see the [Dubai mortgage rates guide](/guides/dubai-mortgage-rates-2026/). The credit score check happens early in the process and can result in a hard stop before other financial analysis even begins.

**Minimum score thresholds** vary by lender and product type, but the market consensus sits between 580 and 620 as the absolute floor for mainstream mortgage consideration. Scores below this typically result in automatic decline at the initial screening stage. This floor has been relatively stable since UAE Central Bank introduced formal mortgage regulations in 2013, though individual bank policies adjust periodically.

**Rate pricing** is directly tied to credit risk in the UAE market. Borrowers with AECB scores above 750 often receive EIBOR margin offers at the lower end of the bank's advertised range. Borrowers with scores between 650 and 699 may face margins 0.25–0.5% higher than the bank's headline rate, which on a AED 2 million mortgage over 20 years translates to tens of thousands of dirhams in additional interest cost over the loan term.

**LTV (Loan-to-Value) ratios** can also be affected. While UAE Central Bank regulations set the regulatory maximums, 80% LTV for expat residents on properties under AED 5 million, individual banks can and do apply stricter in-house LTV caps based on credit score. A borrower with a score of 780 may qualify for the full 80% LTV; a borrower at 640 may find the bank limits them to 70% LTV, requiring a larger down payment.

**Debt-service ratio (DSR) treatment** is another variable. UAE Central Bank caps total debt obligations at 50% of gross monthly salary. Banks assess each obligation against this cap. Some banks are more conservative in how they calculate the DSR for applicants with borderline credit scores, for example, treating revolving credit card limits as fully drawn for calculation purposes, which reduces the apparent mortgage capacity significantly.

For buy-to-let investors and those financing income-producing properties, credit standards can be slightly more flexible on LTV but the minimum score requirements are similar. The [buy-to-let mortgage guide](/guides/buy-to-let-mortgage-dubai/) covers how investment property financing differs from owner-occupier mortgages.

## How to Improve Your UAE Credit Score

Improving an AECB score follows the same logic as any credit score system, but with some UAE-specific considerations. The most impactful changes are always in payment history and credit utilisation, the two highest-weighted factors.

**Pay every bill on time, every month.** This sounds obvious, but the mechanics matter in the UAE. Set up direct debits for your minimum payments on all credit cards and loan EMIs. Credit card minimum payments that are not direct-debited rely on manual transfers, which are often delayed by busy schedules. One 30-day late payment on a credit card can drop a 750 score to below 680 within a single reporting cycle.

**Reduce credit card utilisation to below 30%.** If you have a total credit card limit of AED 50,000 and regularly carry balances totalling AED 30,000, your utilisation is 60%, which is damaging. Pay down balances over 3–6 months before applying for a mortgage. If you can get your utilisation below 20%, the score benefit is meaningful.

**Don't close old accounts.** If you have a credit card that is 4 years old with a clean payment history, keep it open even if you rarely use it. Closing it removes positive history and shortens your average credit age, both of which reduce your score. Put a small recurring charge (fuel, subscriptions) on old cards to keep them active.

**Avoid multiple credit applications before a mortgage.** Every time you apply for a new credit card, personal loan, or car finance, a hard inquiry appears on your report. Multiple hard inquiries within a 90-day window are counted separately, unlike mortgage inquiries in some western markets. In the UAE, each inquiry is a separate event. Stop all new credit applications at least 6 months before your mortgage application.

**Dispute errors promptly.** Errors on UAE credit reports include: closed accounts showing as open, settled debts showing balances, missed payments recorded for payments that went through, accounts that are not yours due to identity mix-ups. File a dispute through the AECB website or app. Banks have 15 business days to respond to AECB disputes; if they do not, the entry is typically removed.

**Build a credit history deliberately.** If you are new to the UAE and have a thin file, a secured credit card, where your credit limit equals a deposit you place with the bank, is the most reliable entry point. Use it for small purchases and pay it in full each month. After 12 months of clean history, the card typically converts to an unsecured product, and your file has 12 months of positive data.

## Common Mistakes That Damage UAE Credit Scores

Several behaviours that are common among expats, particularly those unfamiliar with UAE credit culture, create avoidable score damage.

**Bounced cheques.** Despite 2022 reforms that decriminalised personal bounced cheques, dishonoured cheques are still reported to AECB by banks. A bounced cheque related to a rent payment (post-dated cheques are still used in UAE tenancy arrangements) or a commercial transaction can appear as a derogatory mark. The 2022 reforms removed criminal prosecution risk; they did not remove credit bureau reporting.

**Leaving the country with open balances.** Some expats who leave the UAE assume that credit card balances or personal loan amounts become unenforceable once they depart. This is incorrect. UAE banks report to AECB and, through bilateral agreements with some countries, can pursue outstanding balances internationally. More practically, if you ever return to the UAE or apply for a visa again, any unpaid defaults will be visible.

**Guarantor obligations.** If you have signed as a guarantor for another person's loan in the UAE, that obligation appears on your AECB report. If the primary borrower misses payments, the delinquency can appear on your record as well. most expats are unaware they are guarantors because the guarantee was part of a favour arrangement, understanding what you are signing is critical.

**Minimum payment traps.** Paying only the credit card minimum payment keeps you technically current and avoids late fees, but does not improve your score if utilisation remains high. Worse, UAE credit card interest rates typically run between 36–42% APR on outstanding balances, carrying a balance is financially expensive regardless of the credit score effect.

## Credit Score for Expats and Non-Residents

Expat residents in the UAE build AECB scores in exactly the same way as UAE nationals, the bureau treats all individuals with UAE residency visas identically. The practical challenge for most expats is that credit history from their home country does not transfer to the UAE. A person with a 30-year mortgage history in the UK arrives in the UAE with effectively zero UAE credit history.

This thin-file status does not result in a low score; it results in no score, which many banks treat similarly to a low score for mortgage purposes. The standard advice: open a UAE bank account immediately on arrival, apply for a credit card within the first month, and use it modestly and responsibly for 12–24 months before applying for a mortgage. The [open a bank account in Dubai guide](/guides/open-bank-account-dubai/) walks through which banks are most accessible to new expat arrivals.

Non-residents, those buying UAE property without a UAE residency visa, face additional challenges because they may have no AECB file at all. Non-resident mortgages are offered by some UAE banks but with stricter conditions: typically 25–35% minimum down payment, rates 0.5–1% above the resident equivalent, and income verification requirements that are more demanding. Some non-resident mortgage programmes are specifically designed for HNWI buyers; entry points generally start at AED 3–5 million property values.

For expats comparing banking options across the Gulf, particularly those who may also spend time in Qatar, Bahrain, or Saudi Arabia, credit bureaus operate separately in each jurisdiction. A clean AECB record in the UAE does not automatically translate into a positive credit profile in another GCC country. The [Gulf banking comparison guide](/guides/gulf-banking-comparison-expats/) covers how banking infrastructure and credit systems differ across GCC states.

## Disputing AECB Errors and Correcting Your Report

Errors on AECB reports are more common than most people expect. The most frequently disputed issues include: settled debts still listed as active with outstanding balances; late payment flags that were applied because a payment was a few days late due to a bank processing delay, not non-payment; and accounts that appear on the wrong person's report due to shared employer salary payment systems.

To dispute an entry, log into your AECB account online or through the app and submit a formal dispute. You will need to provide supporting documentation: bank statements showing the payment was made on time, a settlement letter from a lender, or any official correspondence that contradicts what the bureau has on file.

AECB is required to contact the data-contributing institution (the bank or lender) and request a response within 15 business days. If the institution confirms an error, the entry is corrected or removed. If the institution maintains the entry is accurate and you disagree, you can escalate to the UAE Central Bank's consumer protection department.

If you are planning a mortgage application, allow 30–60 days for the dispute and correction process to complete before applying. Applying while a dispute is pending means the lender sees the unresolved entry and may decline or delay.

## Al Etihad Credit Bureau vs International Credit Bureaus

Expats from Europe, North America, or Australia are often familiar with their home-country equivalents, Equifax and Experian in the UK, FICO in the United States, Equifax and Illion in Australia. AECB operates on a similar model but with some differences worth understanding.

The UAE scoring range (300–900) is narrower than the US FICO range (also 300–850) but the score philosophy is comparable: higher is better, above 700 is generally strong, above 750 is competitive. The data that feeds the UAE score skews more heavily toward formal bank credit; informal creditor data, utility payment history outside of telcos, and rental payment records are less consistently included than in some mature credit markets.

The UAE credit ecosystem is younger than Western equivalents, AECB has been fully operational for roughly a decade compared to 50+ years for major US bureaus. This means the predictive models are still maturing, but coverage of formal bank credit is comprehensive.

One important structural difference: UAE banks are legally required to contribute data to AECB and to check AECB before extending credit above a certain threshold. This is mandated by UAE Central Bank regulation, which means there is no opt-out from the system while you hold financial products in the UAE.

## Before You Apply for a UAE Mortgage

A clean and accurate AECB report is the foundation of any successful UAE mortgage application. The practical pre-application checklist for any expat buyer:

- Check your AECB score and full report at least 90 days before applying
- Identify and dispute any errors immediately
- Reduce all credit card balances to below 30% of their limits
- Make no new credit applications for at least 6 months before the mortgage application
- Ensure all existing loan and credit card payments are on direct debit
- Gather 6 months of bank statements from your primary UAE account, lenders will review these alongside the AECB report

For buyers considering buy-to-let investment properties, where rental income will service part of the mortgage, the credit score requirements are similar but the debt-service ratio calculation differs, as banks count projected rental income at a haircut. The [buy-to-let mortgage in Dubai guide](/guides/buy-to-let-mortgage-dubai/) explains how investment property financing works in practice.

Understanding current mortgage pricing before you begin the application process helps you calibrate which lenders are worth approaching given your score. The full breakdown of current rates, EIBOR margins, and how fixed versus variable terms compare is covered in the [Dubai mortgage rates guide](/guides/dubai-mortgage-rates-2026/).

Your AECB score is a number, not a verdict. With deliberate management, consistent payments, controlled utilisation, no speculative credit applications, most expat borrowers can move from a thin file or mid-range score to mortgage-competitive territory within 18–24 months of landing in the UAE.
