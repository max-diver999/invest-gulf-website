Comparisons of Abu Dhabi and Dubai for families are written as a contest of amenities and fees. That framing is useful for a household deciding where to settle from abroad, and misleading for the much more common case: a family already in one emirate that has been offered something in the other.

For them the deciding variable is not which emirate is better. It is that a school seat is not portable between them, and neither is anything attached to it.

<TldrBlock text={"Dubai and Abu Dhabi run separate school regulators, separate admissions and separate inspection frameworks, so an inter-emirate move forfeits the seat, the sibling priority that came with it and, in practice, the deposit. ## What exactly is lost in the move?

Three things, and only the first is obvious. The seat itself, which was granted by a school under one regulator and has no standing under the other. The sibling priority that seat conferred on younger children. And the deposit, which secured a place the family is choosing to leave.

The second is the expensive one. A household with children at one campus experiences admissions as a solved problem: the eldest got in, and the others followed on a rule the school applies to existing families. Crossing the emirate boundary dissolves that rule and converts one solved problem into as many separate problems as there are children. Each competes independently for whatever is available in its own year group, and the year groups do not have the same availability. Families reliably plan for the eldest's transfer and discover the youngest's afterwards.

## Why does the calendar outrank the job offer?

Because seats are released on an intake cycle and salaries are not. An employment start date is a negotiation between two parties who both want it to work; a place in year four is a fixed quantity that either exists in September or does not.

The asymmetry is worth being blunt about. A job can usually absorb a delay of some weeks, a remote start, or a period of commuting. A school year cannot be entered halfway on the same terms: the seats remaining in February are the ones nobody took in September, which is a selection rather than a sample. Families who let the employment date drive the move take that selection and live with it for a year, having optimised the variable that was flexible and accepted the constraint on the one that was not. The wider cost and lifestyle comparison between the two emirates sits on the [Dubai versus Abu Dhabi living page](/guides/dubai-vs-abu-dhabi-living/).

## What does the paperwork actually cost?

More than relocation budgets allow, because the costs are per child and they arrive on both sides of the move.

| Item | Where it lands | Per child |
|---|---|---|
| Outgoing deposit | Forfeited on departure | Yes |
| Incoming deposit | Payable at the new school | Yes |
| Registration and assessment fees | New school, at application | Yes |
| Transfer certificate and records | Outgoing school, administrative | Yes |
| Uniform, books, transport setup | New school, first term | Yes |

None of these is large in isolation and all of them multiply. A family of three moving between emirates is paying six deposits across the two ends of the transaction plus three sets of everything else, at the same time as it is paying a new tenancy, a new agency fee and a move. This is the point at which a relocation package that looked adequate stops being adequate, and it is entirely predictable in advance. Fee levels themselves and how the two regulators handle increases are set out on the [school fees comparison](/guides/dubai-vs-abu-dhabi-school-fees/).

## Can you compare the two regulators' ratings?

Not directly, and treating them as equivalent produces a comparison that feels rigorous and is not. Each emirate inspects on its own framework, with its own criteria, published on its own scale.

What that means for a family is that a school rated highly in one emirate and a school rated highly in the other have each satisfied a different assessor asking somewhat different questions. Both ratings are real and both are useful within their own system; neither converts. The practical approach is to use each rating to rank schools within its own emirate, and to compare across the boundary on things that are actually comparable: the curriculum, the year-group size, the staff turnover, the actual campus visited on an ordinary day. The frameworks themselves are explained on the [KHDA ratings page](/guides/khda-school-ratings-explained/) and the [ADEK ratings page](/guides/adek-school-ratings-abu-dhabi/).

## What carries across the boundary?

The curriculum carries across, and very little else does. A child in a British stream continues in a British stream; an IB student continues in IB. That continuity is the reason inter-emirate moves work at all for school-age children.

It is also the thing to hold fixed while treating everything else as open. Families who begin the search with a school in mind, usually the counterpart of the one they are leaving, narrow the field before they know what is available and often end up choosing between two poor options in the same brand. Families who begin with the curriculum and search the whole emirate for seats in the right year groups end up with more choices and a better one. The sequence of an arrival more generally, for households doing this from abroad rather than across the boundary, is covered on the [moving with family page](/guides/moving-to-dubai-with-family/).

## Why does the school search precede the housing search?

Because one of the two is a constrained resource and the other is not. Housing in both emirates is continuously available at every price point; seats in a specific year group at a specific school are not available at all until they are.

Reversing the order is the most common structural mistake in these moves, and it is made for an understandable reason: the tenancy has a deadline attached and the school does not feel like it does. A family signs a lease near the new office, then searches for schools reachable from it, and discovers the reachable set is small. They have optimised a commute at the cost of the year group's options, and the resulting school run is the one they will do twice a day for as long as they live there. Doing it the other way costs nothing and constrains nothing: find the seats, then choose a home that serves them, which in both emirates is a wide field.

## What does the assessment stage add?

An unknown, and one that lands late. Receiving schools assess incoming children before confirming a place, and an assessment can produce a year-group placement different from the one the family expected.

The mechanism is ordinary and the timing is what hurts. A child moving between curricula, or between two schools teaching the same curriculum at different paces, can be offered a place in the year below or asked to demonstrate readiness for the year above. Where that happens, the family is renegotiating the whole plan at the point where the tenancy is signed and the notice at the old school is served. Building a fortnight of slack into the sequence, between assessment outcome and any irreversible commitment, costs nothing when the assessment is routine and is the only thing that helps when it is not.

Two children in the same family can also land differently, which is the version nobody plans for: one confirmed at the target school and one offered a place elsewhere. That outcome is survivable and it doubles the school run, so it is worth asking each school what it does when a sibling pair splits before it happens rather than after.

## When is a move worth refusing?

When the seats do not exist in the year groups that matter and the offer cannot wait for the next intake.

That is a real answer rather than a rhetorical one, and it is worth having ready before the conversation with an employer, because the question tends to be asked in a form that assumes only money is negotiable. What a family in this position is actually asking for is a start date aligned to a term boundary, or a period of commuting, or a temporary arrangement in which one parent moves first. All three are ordinary requests. None of them is available after a tenancy has been signed and a term has been missed, which is why the seat search belongs at the start of the negotiation rather than after it.
