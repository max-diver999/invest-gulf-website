Saudi Arabia's foreign ownership framework is usually described as a list of places that opened. That description is accurate and almost useless at the point of purchase, because designation attaches to mapped boundaries released in phases, and a boundary runs between plots rather than around cities.

The question a buyer has to answer is narrower than "is Riyadh open". It is whether this plot, on this date, sits inside a released phase of an approved zone, and whether anything operating inside that zone closes it anyway.

And no use-based carve-out applies to it, because designation opens an area subject to exclusions that keep operating inside it. Makkah and Madinah sit outside all of this permanently: that exclusion is categorical, not a phase waiting its turn."} />

## Why does a city name tell you nothing?

Because designation is drawn as a boundary and a boundary has two sides. Two towers on the same street, sharing a postcode and a view, can sit on opposite sides of a line that no one standing between them can see.

Marketing operates at the level a buyer recognises, which is the city or the district. Eligibility operates at the level of a surveyed parcel. The gap between those two resolutions is where the entire risk in this market sits, and it is invisible in every artefact a buyer normally receives: the listing, the brochure, the site visit, the neighbouring completed sale. None of those is wrong about anything. They simply answer a question at a scale coarser than the one that decides the transaction. The market-level position, including what changed in 2026 and what it costs, is on the [Saudi property for foreigners guide](/saudi-arabia/); this page is about turning an address into a boundary record.

## What is the difference between an approved zone and a released phase?

Approval defines an area in principle. Release makes a specific phase of it available. Sales material collapses the two because both are true statements about the same masterplan, and a buyer reading it has no way to tell which one applies to the unit in front of them.

| What you are shown | What it establishes | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Giga-project masterplan | The development exists and is national policy | Whether any of it is released |
| Zone approval announcement | An area is designated in principle | Which phases within it are live |
| Phase release decision | A specific mapped phase is available | Whether your plot is inside that phase |
| Boundary record for a plot | The plot's position against the released line | Use-based carve-outs, checked separately |

The pattern that costs money is a masterplan drawn across an area containing both released and unreleased phases. It is one drawing, it is honest, and it does not distinguish. A buyer is shown the whole and sold a part, with nothing in the room indicating which part. Off-plan makes this sharper, because off-plan is sold ahead of ground conditions by design and there is nothing physically present to contradict a drawing. Structural risks in that segment are covered on the [Saudi off-plan guide](/guides/saudi-off-plan-guide/).

## What survives inside an approved zone?

Carve-outs that operate on use rather than on location, and they keep operating after designation. A plot can sit comfortably inside a released phase and still be unavailable.

The recurring categories are land reserved for public or government purposes, parcels carrying a religious or heritage designation, and strategic or security-adjacent land. None of these announces itself in a listing, and a plot subject to one of them looks exactly like the plot beside it that is free. The mental model that keeps buyers out of trouble is that designation opens a boundary within which a further set of questions begins. It does not answer them. A buyer who treats zone approval as the end of the eligibility enquiry has stopped one question early, at precisely the point where the remaining question is invisible from the street.

## Which exclusion is permanent rather than pending?

The one covering Makkah and Madinah. It is categorical, and reading it as a sequencing decision is the most expensive available misunderstanding in this market.

Every other closed area in Saudi Arabia can be described as not yet designated, and that phrasing is correct: the map has grown steadily and there is a mechanism by which it grows further. The two holy cities are outside that mechanism. Foreign interests there take the form of leases and usufruct rights of limited term rather than ownership, which is a different instrument with a different exit and a different valuation. Buyers arrive at this question by analogy, having watched Riyadh and Jeddah phases release on a schedule, and conclude that patience is the strategy. It is not a queue, and no amount of waiting advances a position in it.

## How do you actually get the record?

Through Saudi-qualified counsel instructed on a plot number, asking for the boundary and phase record and the release decision for the phase it names. Two documents, requested together, because either one alone is answerable and misleading.

What does not substitute for them:

- A developer's zone map, which is a drawing prepared for sales rather than an extract from a register.
- A completed neighbouring sale, which establishes that a different plot was eligible on a different date.
- A confirmation that the project is approved, which answers the first of three questions.
- A map dated more than a year ago, which describes a smaller footprint than the current one and may omit either the phase that opened your plot or the phase that did not.

The date on the document carries as much information as its content, because this map grows by release. An old map reads as authoritative in both directions and is reliable in neither.

## Where does this leave costs?

At the end of the sequence rather than the beginning. Transfer costs apply to a transaction that can lawfully complete, so pricing a purchase before its eligibility is settled is pricing something that may not exist.

That ordering matters more here than in markets where eligibility is a formality, because the failure is not a worse deal but an absent one. Buyers commonly arrive with a fee schedule assembled, a financing plan, and a modelled yield, having spent nothing on the one question that determines whether any of it applies. The yield modelling itself is worth doing, and the rent side is set out on the [Saudi rental yield guide](/guides/saudi-rental-yield-guide/), but it belongs after the boundary record rather than before it. Residency thresholds, which are a separate capital question again, are on the [Premium Residency page](/guides/saudi-premium-residency-property/).

## Does buying from a foreign owner settle the question?

It settles a different question from the one buyers ask. A foreign seller holding registered title proves that this parcel was open to a foreign buyer on the day that seller registered it, which is evidence about a date rather than about today.

Two things can have moved since. The carve-out position can have changed, because reservations for public or strategic use are applied to parcels rather than granted to persons. And the seller's own registration may have been completed under a phase arrangement that has since been superseded. Neither is common, and neither is what usually goes wrong on a resale. What makes the point worth stating is the confidence a foreign-owned title inspires: it reads as proof that the eligibility question has been asked and answered by somebody else, and it is the one document in a resale file that a buyer will accept without reading the date on it.

## What does a clean file look like?

Four documents, and the useful test is whether a stranger could reconstruct your eligibility from them without asking you anything.

1. The boundary and phase record for the plot number, dated within the current phase map.
2. The release decision for the phase that record names.
3. Written confirmation that no use-based carve-out attaches to the parcel.
4. The seller's own title, establishing that the person selling holds what the record describes.

A file with the first two and not the third is the most common shape, and it is the one that reads as complete. It establishes that the plot is in an open area and says nothing about whether the plot itself is open. Where a buyer is working with counsel who has produced the first two quickly and cheaply, the third is the question worth pressing, because it is the one that requires someone to look at the parcel rather than at the map. Day-to-day ownership and running costs, once all four are in hand, sit on the [foreigner living guide](/guides/saudi-property-foreigner-living/).
