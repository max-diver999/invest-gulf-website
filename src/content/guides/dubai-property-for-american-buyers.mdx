Every guide written for a foreign buyer in Dubai spends its energy on currency: when to convert, how to hedge, what a move in the rate does to a payment plan. For an American buyer that entire chapter is empty.

The dirham has been pegged at 3.6725 to the dollar for decades. A Dubai price is therefore a dollar price, fixed, with no timing decision attached to it. That makes the American buyer the only significant foreign cohort in this market with nothing to decide about exchange rates, and it means the difficulty in an American purchase has moved somewhere else entirely: to the sending side.

## What does the peg actually remove?

A decision, and with it a whole category of advice. A buyer paying in a floating currency has to choose when to convert, whether to fix a rate forward, and how to handle instalments falling due across a payment plan. None of that applies here.

The practical effect is that an American buyer can price a Dubai property once and have that price stay true. A unit at AED 2.2 million is USD 599,000 today and USD 599,000 at the trustee appointment, and an off-plan payment plan running across 3 years carries no currency assumption inside it at all. A sterling buyer looking at the same unit is holding an open position for the length of the plan. This is a genuine structural advantage rather than a presentational one, and it is worth stating plainly because a buyer who does not know it is true will pay for hedging they do not need. The peg as a market fact, including how it behaves against other currencies, is on the [currency page](/guides/currency-exchange-aed-usd-eur/).

## Where does the difficulty go instead?

Into the wire. The gating constraint in an American purchase is not the price, the property or the paperwork in Dubai. It is getting a large sum out of a US bank account and into a UAE trustee account without a hold.

| Stage | Who reviews it | What they want |
|---|---|---|
| Origination | Your own US bank | Purpose, counterparty, source of funds |
| Correspondent | An intermediary bank | A coherent payment with a named beneficiary |
| Receiving | The UAE bank holding the trustee account | Matching transaction reference |
| Settlement | The registered trustee | Cleared funds on the appointment date |

Nothing in that chain is designed to obstruct a property purchase, and the great majority complete without incident. The exposure is timing rather than refusal: a review at any stage adds days, and the date it collides with is a trustee appointment that has a seller and a registration slot attached to it. That is the specific reason American buyers should treat the wire as a scheduled workstream with its own lead time rather than as the last administrative step.

## How do you make the wire uneventful?

You do it by telling the bank first. A transfer the bank is expecting is a scheduled review; the same transfer arriving unannounced is an exception, and exceptions are what get held.

The conversation is short and worth having 4 to 6 weeks before the money is needed. Give the amount, the destination institution, the purpose, and the counterparty, and ask what documentation they want on file. Most banks will name two or three items: the sale agreement, evidence of the source of funds, and the trustee's details. Having those on file in advance converts a compliance query into a lookup. It also surfaces any institution-specific limit before it matters, which occasionally sends a buyer to a different bank, and that is a discovery worth making a month early rather than on a Tuesday with a Wednesday appointment.

One structural point helps here. Dubai transfers settle through registered trustee offices rather than by paying a seller directly, so the beneficiary is a regulated intermediary tied to a specific transaction. That is a materially easier thing to explain to a compliance officer than a payment to a private individual overseas, and it is worth saying explicitly rather than assuming the bank will infer it. How the trustee stage works in a remote purchase is set out on the [remote buying page](/guides/how-to-buy-dubai-property-remotely/).

## What is different about financing as an American?

What blocks it is the absence of a record. Non-resident lending in the UAE exists on tighter terms than resident lending, and the specific American difficulty is that a lender is underwriting a borrower with no UAE credit file at all.

| What the lender sees | Resident borrower | American non-resident |
|---|---|---|
| UAE credit file | Present, and decisive | Absent entirely |
| Income currency | AED, matching the loan | USD, converted at a fixed peg |
| Minimum deposit | From 20% | Higher, and lender-specific |
| Maximum term | Longer | Shorter |
| Assessment time | Weeks | Longer, with more documents |

That changes what the file looks like rather than whether it can be done. The bank is assessing dollar income, US employment or business documentation, and assets held outside its own market, without the local repayment history it would ordinarily lean on. In practice this means more documents, a longer assessment, and a lender that will want the picture to be simple: straightforward income, clean statements, and a purchase that does not require explanation. Buyers with complex compensation, or income routed through entities, should expect the file to take longer than the property timeline allows unless it is started early. Deposit tiers and the general non-resident position are on the [non-resident mortgage page](/guides/non-resident-mortgage-dubai/), and the full closing-cost stack on the [cost of buying page](/guides/cost-of-buying-property-dubai/).

## Does the power of attorney chain fit the timeline?

Only if it starts at the offer rather than at the transfer. Buying remotely is routine in Dubai and rests on a power of attorney granted to someone locally to sign and attend the trustee appointment, but the document has to be notarised in the US and then legalised for UAE use.

That chain runs through offices with their own queues and takes 3 to 6 weeks in ordinary conditions, against a Dubai resale that can complete in 4 to 8. American buyers consistently begin it once the financial arrangements feel settled, which is several weeks later than they should. The sequence that works is to treat the power of attorney as the first administrative act after an offer is accepted, in parallel with the bank conversation, so that the two long-lead items run alongside each other instead of end to end.

## What does the whole timeline look like?

Four workstreams, and the two long ones have to start first. Laid against each other rather than in sequence, an American purchase from offer to keys runs comfortably; laid end to end, it runs late.

1. Power of attorney, notarised and legalised: 3 to 6 weeks, starting at offer acceptance.
2. Bank pre-notification and documentation: 4 to 6 weeks of lead time, starting the same day.
3. Mortgage file, where financing is used: longer than the property timeline unless begun immediately.
4. The transaction itself: offer, sale agreement, trustee appointment, registration.

The mistake that costs American buyers the most is treating the first two as administration to be handled once the commercial terms are settled. They are not administration; they are the critical path. A buyer who opens both on the day an offer is accepted arrives at the trustee appointment with the money cleared and the signature authority in place. A buyer who waits until the appointment is booked discovers that neither the bank nor the notary works to a property calendar.

## What is deliberately not on this page?

Tax. An American buying property abroad has a set of obligations that follow from citizenship rather than from residence, and they are substantial enough that folding them into a payments article would do them badly.

The short version is that the peg simplifies your purchase and simplifies nothing about your filing position: worldwide income reporting continues, foreign asset disclosure applies on its own thresholds, and there is no US-UAE income tax treaty to lean on. Those questions belong with a US adviser and are set out on the [US citizens tax page](/guides/tax-us-citizens-dubai/). The reason to name them here rather than ignore them is that the ease of the currency side gives some American buyers the impression the whole transaction is simpler than it is. The purchase is simpler. The tax position is not, and it runs on a different calendar than the trustee appointment.
