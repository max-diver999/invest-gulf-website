## The 10 etiquette rules that matter most in Qatar

Cover shoulders and knees in public buildings, keep swimwear at the pool, respect Ramadan fasting hours in public, ask before photographing anyone, and accept the coffee you are offered. Those five habits prevent almost every awkward moment a visitor is likely to have. Qatar is internationally connected and majority expat, and West Bay, The Pearl and Lusail feel cosmopolitan, yet public behaviour still follows local law, Islamic custom and a strong expectation of personal privacy.

| Situation | The short rule |
|---|---|
| Malls, souqs, government offices | Shoulders and knees covered, no beachwear |
| Ramadan daylight hours | No eating, drinking, gum or smoking in public view |
| Alcohol | Licensed venues only, never in public, never before driving |
| Photography | Ask first, never Qatari women or children, never security sites |
| Greetings | Let a Qatari woman offer her hand first |
| Hospitality | Accept dates and Arabic coffee, a polite sip is enough |
| Public affection | Handholding at most, nothing more |
| Online posts | No mocking Islam, no naming and shaming, no defamation |
| Tipping | Welcome, not obligatory, round up or give a few riyals |
| When unsure | Watch what Qataris nearby are doing, then copy it |

Do not assume that because most residents are foreign, the rules do not apply. Enforcement can feel selective right up to the moment it is not, and the consequences range from a polite word from a security guard to fines, employer discipline or, in extreme cases, deportation. Getting this right is not about performing piety, it is about behaving like a considerate guest in a country where most of the people you meet are also guests.

## What can women wear in Qatar, and are shorts allowed?

Women should cover shoulders and knees in malls, souqs and government buildings, which in practice means a t-shirt or blouse with trousers, a midi skirt or a long dress. Short shorts and cropped tops draw disapproval in those settings, and some malls display signs asking for respectful dress. Around The Pearl, West Bay hotels and expat compounds the mood is far more relaxed, and shorts on a weekend brunch terrace are unremarkable, so the sensible approach is to carry a light shawl and switch registers depending on where the day takes you.

Abayas are not mandatory for non-Muslim women in daily life. Many expat women own one anyway, either to blend in on official errands or because loose black fabric is genuinely cooler than it looks in August. For most mosque visits an abaya or long loose clothing plus a headscarf is expected, and shoes come off at the door.

| Setting | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Malls and souqs | Shoulders covered, knees covered | Shoulders and knees covered, avoid deep necklines |
| Government offices and MOI | Long trousers, covered shoulders | Abaya not required for expats, conservative dress advised |
| Public family beaches | Swim shorts acceptable | One piece or modest bikini, never topless |
| Hotel pools and beach clubs | Standard swimwear | Standard swimwear within club rules |
| The Pearl and West Bay dining | Relaxed, but not beachwear at the table | Relaxed, carry a shawl for the mall afterwards |
| Mosque visits | Long trousers, remove shoes | Headscarf plus abaya or long loose clothing, remove shoes |

## What should men wear in Qatar?

For men the baseline is simple: long trousers and a shirt that covers the shoulders will take you anywhere in Qatar without comment. Shorts are fine at the marina, at the beach, at the gym and in expat neighbourhoods, and they are a poor choice for government buildings, traditional souqs and any meeting where you want to be taken seriously. Vests and open shirts in malls attract the same quiet disapproval as beachwear.

Business dress runs formal by regional standards. Corporate towers in West Bay expect a suit or smart business casual, government meetings expect the suit, and dressing slightly above the room is read as respect rather than showing off. Fridays are quiet, with many offices closed or on half days.

## How do you behave during Ramadan in Qatar?

During Ramadan you must not eat, drink, chew gum or smoke in public view during daylight hours, and this applies to every resident and visitor regardless of religion. You can eat and drink freely at home, in a screened office kitchen and in hotel venues that operate behind screening for non-fasting guests. Water in a meeting room is not a small liberty here, it is the single most common mistake newcomers make, so keep the bottle in your bag until you are somewhere private.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat and drink inside your home, a closed office kitchen, or designated hotel areas | Eat, drink, chew gum, or smoke in public view during daylight |
| Accept iftar invitations and break the fast at sunset with colleagues | Play loud music in residential areas during prayer times |
| Shorten meetings and expect lower afternoon energy | Push fasting colleagues into lunch meetings without an alternative |
| Dress more modestly than you usually would | Wear revealing clothing in malls or souqs |
| Use the greeting "Ramadan Kareem" if you are comfortable with it | Joke about fasting or about how tired fasting colleagues look |

Office culture shifts for the month. Many employers move to shorter hours, often something like 9am to 3pm, so plan work that needs decisions into the morning. Roads spike in the 30 to 60 minutes before maghrib as everyone races home for iftar, which is the one time of year when leaving the office early is the safer choice rather than the lazy one.

### Iftar and suhoor

Iftar, the meal that breaks the fast at sunset, is the social centre of the month. Employers, neighbours, schools and hotels host iftar tents, and accepting an invitation is a genuine investment in the relationship rather than a courtesy. Arrive on time, wait for the call to prayer before eating unless your host says otherwise, and try the dates and Arabic coffee that open most tables. Suhoor, the pre dawn meal, is quieter, with hotel buffets running late into the night. If you are staying in a hotel, remember that other guests are eating at 4am and sleeping afterwards.

### Eid al-Fitr

Eid is a multi day public holiday. Government offices close, schools break, and travel demand peaks in both directions. The greeting is "Eid Mubarak". Gift giving is common among Qatari families, and expats usually send messages to close colleagues rather than presents. If you are timing a move, avoid the first week of Ramadan and the Eid travel crush, and read the [Qatar relocation guide](/guides/qatar-relocation-guide/) before you fix dates with a shipper.

## What are the rules on alcohol in Qatar?

Alcohol is restricted rather than banned. Licensed hotels, restaurants and clubs serve it legally, and residents can buy from the Qatar Distribution Company with a permit tied to their salary and sponsor approval. What is illegal is drinking or being drunk in public, carrying alcohol openly outside licensed premises, and driving with any measurable alcohol in your blood. Treat that last point as absolute, because there is no social tolerance for a single glass before the drive home.

Alcohol is generally not sold in supermarkets, and it should never appear as a gift for a Muslim household unless you know the family well and know they drink. Public drunkenness carries reputational and legal risk that is out of proportion to the fun involved, and it is the fastest way for an expat to attract exactly the wrong kind of official attention. Verify current permit rules and venue licensing when you arrive, since both change with policy and with ownership.

## Do you tip in Qatar?

Tipping is appreciated but never obligatory in Qatar. Restaurants often add a service charge, and when they do, rounding the bill up is perfectly adequate. Where no service charge appears, roughly 10 percent is a normal gesture for good table service.

| Service | Typical gesture |
|---|---|
| Restaurant with service charge on the bill | Round up, nothing more expected |
| Restaurant without service charge | Around 10 percent if the service was good |
| Taxi and ride hailing | Round up to the nearest note |
| Hotel porter and valet | A few riyals in cash |
| Grocery and food delivery | A few riyals, more in summer heat |
| Salon, spa and barber | Around 10 percent |
| Building watchman and cleaner | Small cash at Eid, common among residents |

Cash still works better than card for tips, so keep small notes. Many of the people serving you are supporting families abroad, and a modest, consistent tip lands better than an occasional grand one.

## Can you take photos in Qatar?

Ask before you photograph anyone, and never photograph Qatari women or children without explicit permission. This is treated as a serious matter culturally and legally, not as a minor faux pas. Skyline shots from the Corniche, your own family at expat venues, food and interiors with venue permission, and signposted tourist sites are all fine.

| Subject | Risk |
|---|---|
| Qatari women and children | Serious offence, both cultural and legal sensitivity |
| Government, military and security buildings | Fines, equipment confiscation |
| Airport security zones | Prohibited |
| Strangers in souqs | Ask first, gesture at the camera, wait for the nod |
| Accidents and police scenes | Do not photograph, do not circulate |

Social media raises the stakes rather than lowering them. Posting identifiable images of other people without consent, especially Qatari nationals, creates both reputational and legal exposure, and blurring backgrounds is a cheap insurance policy. Drones require licensing and recreational flying is heavily regulated, so verify current official rules and never fly without authorisation.

## How does Qatari hospitality work if you are invited home?

Accept what is offered, and accept it with your right hand. Dates and Arabic coffee, qahwa, are the opening ritual of almost every Qatari welcome, and waving them away twice reads as a rejection of the host rather than a dietary preference. A polite sip is enough if you do not want more, and gently shaking the small cup signals that you are finished.

A few house rules save embarrassment. Remove your shoes if your hosts do. Compliment the home sincerely and briefly rather than effusively. Avoid pointing the sole of your foot at anyone when seated on floor cushions. Bring quality dates, chocolates or flowers, and never alcohol unless you know the household. Many homes still separate the men's majlis from the family area, so follow your host's lead rather than assuming a mixed room. Expat compound life runs on Western hospitality norms instead, with barbecues and school events, and the same reciprocity rule applies: return the invitation.

## How do men and women interact in public in Qatar?

Keep public affection minimal. Handholding between married couples passes without comment in most settings, and anything beyond that is out of place in malls, souqs and family areas. In greetings, let a Qatari woman extend her hand first, since many prefer not to shake hands with men, and a hand placed on your own chest with a nod is a respectful alternative that never offends.

Mixed social life is entirely normal in offices, hotels and expat venues, and women work, drive and lead teams across every sector. Qatari law criminalises same sex sexual activity, and public expression of same sex relationships carries legal and safety risk. That is stated here as a planning fact for anyone weighing a move, not as a judgment. Reforms since 2020 relaxed cohabitation enforcement in many cases, but employer housing policies can still restrict it, so verify current local rules with your HR team rather than relying on what a friend experienced two years ago.

## Which Arabic phrases actually help in Qatar?

English covers everything an expat needs in Qatar, from hospitals to banks to school admissions, so learning Arabic is a courtesy rather than a necessity. About ten phrases will noticeably warm up interactions with Qatari colleagues and with the service staff you see every day.

- "As-salamu alaykum" as a formal greeting, answered with "wa alaykum as-salam"
- "Marhaba" for a casual hello
- "Shukran" for thank you, "afwan" for you are welcome
- "Insha'Allah" for anything scheduled in the future
- "Khalas" for finished or that is enough
- "Ramadan Kareem" during the holy month
- "Eid Mubarak" during Eid
- "Yalla" for let us go
- "Min fadlak" or "min fadlik" for please
- "Ma'a as-salama" for goodbye

## How does religion shape daily life in Qatar?

Five daily prayers structure the day, and the call to prayer is audible from mosques across the city. In most modern towers it is a background sound rather than a disruption. If a colleague steps out to the office prayer room, pause the conversation and pick it up afterwards. Friday is the weekly holy day and the midday Jumu'ah prayer is its centre, so many businesses close Friday morning or the full day, and Thursday evening carries the social energy of a Friday night elsewhere.

Registered churches serve Christian communities in designated compounds, and private worship is permitted, while public proselytising is prohibited. Verify current official rules for your own denomination before you arrive. The one line that should never be crossed is disrespect toward Islam in speech, in social media or in public, and that includes crude jokes about fasting, the Quran or the Prophet.

## Is Qatar safe for visitors and families?

Qatar is consistently among the safest countries in the region for street level crime, and residents routinely describe walking in Doha at night, leaving a phone on a café table or letting older children take a taxi as ordinary rather than reckless. Petty theft exists but is rare, and violent crime against expats is uncommon. Verify current advisories from your own foreign ministry before travel, since general safety and specific advice are different things.

The genuine risks are mundane. Traffic is the main physical danger, summer heat between June and September is a health risk rather than a discomfort, and the legal environment around defamation, cybercrime and public behaviour is stricter than most Western visitors expect. Criticising Qatar, an employer or a neighbour publicly online is legally risky in a way that criticising them privately is not. For medical logistics, health cover and emergency numbers, see the [Qatar healthcare guide](/guides/qatar-healthcare-guide/).

## When is the best time to visit Qatar?

November to March is the comfortable season, with daytime temperatures that make the Corniche, desert trips and outdoor dining genuinely pleasant. That window is also when hotel rates and event calendars peak, so book earlier than you would in summer.

| Period | What to expect |
|---|---|
| November to March | Coolest months, outdoor season, highest hotel demand |
| April and October | Warm shoulder months, good value, still workable outdoors |
| May to September | Very hot and humid, indoor life, cheapest hotel rates |
| Ramadan (moves each year) | Reduced daytime dining, striking evening atmosphere, verify dates |

Ramadan falls on the lunar calendar and shifts about eleven days earlier each year, so confirm the dates for your travel year. Visiting during Ramadan is rewarding if you want to see the country at its most social after sunset, and frustrating if you expect a normal daytime restaurant scene.

## How do souqs and bargaining work in Doha?

Bargaining is expected in Souq Waqif and the gold souq, and it is unwelcome in malls and chain stores where prices are fixed. The tone matters more than the technique: friendly, unhurried, willing to walk away without drama. Accept the tea or coffee if it is offered, because that is the transaction becoming a conversation rather than a sales tactic.

Ask for prices in writing for anything substantial, especially gold, carpets and furniture, and keep receipts for warranty claims. Cash still gives you the strongest negotiating position in the traditional souqs, while card is universal everywhere else.

## What is driving like in Doha?

Expect assertive lane discipline, fast merges and heavy camera enforcement. Speed and red light cameras are widespread, fines attach to the vehicle and can block registration renewal, and any measurable alcohol behind the wheel is treated as a serious offence rather than a minor one. Verify current fine schedules and licence conversion rules with the relevant authority when you arrive, and start with the [Qatar driving licence guide](/guides/qatar-driving-license/).

Two local patterns are worth planning around. Roads spike before iftar during Ramadan, and school run traffic in West Bay, The Pearl and Lusail compresses into narrow windows morning and afternoon. If you are choosing where to live, commute time between your home and the school gate matters more than most people expect, which is why families often compare [Doha rent by area](/guides/doha-rent-prices-by-area/) alongside school shortlists.

## What is business etiquette like with Qatari colleagues?

Relationships come before process. A first meeting that produces coffee, questions about your family and no decisions is not a failed meeting, it is the normal opening move, and pushing for a signature in that room is the fastest way to lose the room. Expect hierarchy to matter, expect the most senior person to speak last, and expect written follow up to be valued once verbal agreement exists.

Practical rhythm points: schedule important meetings in the morning, avoid Friday entirely, expect shorter hours during Ramadan, and treat "insha'Allah" as genuine uncertainty about timing rather than a soft yes. Direct communication is respected, and public criticism of a colleague is not. Dress formally, arrive on time even if your counterpart does not, and keep your first meeting agenda short.

## What is expat life in Qatar actually like?

Expat life in Qatar is comfortable, well serviced and socially organised around compounds, schools, workplaces and nationality groups rather than neighbourhoods in the European sense. Most people find their circle within the first three months through work, their children's school or sport and hobby clubs, and the small size of Doha means the same faces recur quickly. The Pearl, West Bay and Lusail concentrate the cosmopolitan version of that life, with waterfront dining, gyms and international schools within short drives.

The honest tradeoffs are seasonal and structural. Summer confines outdoor life for four months, family life depends on membership of clubs and compounds with pools, and career progression often depends on your employer rather than an open job market. Many residents also arrive on a two year plan and stay for eight, which is why housing decisions taken as temporary end up mattering. ## Which topics should you avoid discussing?

Regional politics, the internal affairs of the ruling family, religion as debate rather than as fact, Israel and Palestine, and labour conditions are all conversations where a visitor has more to lose than to gain. This is not because Qataris are humourless about their country, it is because the legal environment around public criticism is strict and because you rarely know who is in the room.

Two other habits travel badly. Comparing Qatar unfavourably to Dubai in front of Qatari colleagues reads as rudeness rather than analysis. Complaining publicly online about an employer, a landlord or a service provider carries defamation and cybercrime risk, so raise disputes through the proper channel and vent privately.

## What do new arrivals in Qatar get wrong most often?

The pattern our relocation conversations keep surfacing is that people prepare for the big cultural questions and get caught by the small logistical ones. Nobody forgets that Ramadan exists, and plenty of people forget that their water bottle is visible in a meeting room. Nobody plans to photograph a stranger's children, and plenty of people raise a phone at a souq stall without thinking. The three recurring gaps we see are dress that works for the compound but not for a government office, a household that treats alcohol permits as an afterthought until a first shipment arrives, and school commute times that were never tested at 7:30am on a weekday.

The fix in each case is boring and effective. Keep one conservative outfit ready for official errands, sort the permit and insurance paperwork in your first month, and drive the school route before you sign a lease.
