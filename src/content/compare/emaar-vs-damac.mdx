Comparisons of these two developers are comparisons of product. Which builds better communities, which finishes to a higher standard, which offers the more generous payment plan. Reasonable questions about a house.

An off-plan purchase is not primarily a purchase of a house. For three years it is an unsecured advance to a company, and the question that governs it is what condition that company is in.

Where it does not, the only available proxy is a backward-looking delivery record, and the delivery percentages that circulate have no published methodology behind them. ## What are you actually holding for three years?

What you hold is a contract and a receipt. Until handover you own a claim on a company's future performance, not an apartment, and the value of a claim depends on who is on the other side of it.

This reframing changes what belongs in the diligence. A house buyer asks about layouts, finishes and the view. A creditor asks whether the borrower can pay, what else they owe, how their revenue is trending and what happens to them in a downturn. An off-plan buyer is doing both jobs at once and almost always does only the first, because the sales process is built entirely around the first and provides no natural moment to ask the second. The general shape of that exposure is set out on the [off-plan property guide](/guides/off-plan-property-dubai-guide/).

## Why does public filing change the analysis?

Because it converts an opinion into a document. A company that files periodic accounts publishes a balance sheet, a cash position and a set of obligations on a fixed timetable, and anyone can read them.

That is not a guarantee of anything. It is an input, and it is the input that separates forming a view from accepting one. A buyer who can read the accounts can ask whether the company's commitments look proportionate to its resources, watch whether that changes between one filing and the next, and act on what they see while their remaining instalments are still unpaid. A buyer with no filings has none of that. They have a brand, a sales office, and whatever consensus has formed in the market, and consensus about a private company is not evidence about a private company. Listing status changes over time, so confirm the current position for the developer in front of you rather than relying on this page.

## What about the delivery percentages everyone quotes?

They are worth discarding entirely, and here is why. Figures of the form "ninety-five per cent on time" against "eighty-two per cent" appear across the property internet, including on earlier versions of this page, and none of them carries a source.

| What a usable delivery statistic needs | What the circulating figures have |
|---|---|
| A definition of "on time" | None stated |
| A named sample of projects | None stated |
| A publisher who stands behind it | None identified |
| A date range | None stated |
| A method for handling cancelled projects | Unaddressed |

A number with none of those properties is not weak evidence; it is decoration. What can be assembled instead is specific and slower: take the developer's completed projects in the same segment, find the announced handover date at launch and the actual one, and read the difference. That is a small dataset built by hand, and it is worth more than a percentage nobody can trace. The method is set out on the [how to evaluate a developer page](/guides/how-to-evaluate-dubai-developer/).

## Does escrow not make this moot?

It addresses a different failure from this one. Escrow exists so that money paid for one project is spent on that project rather than diverted, and that protection is real and worth having.

The failure it does not address is the one this page is about. A developer who applies every dirham correctly and still cannot finish leaves buyers holding an incomplete building, and the escrow account holds nothing because the money was properly spent on construction that stopped short. Recovery in that situation is a process rather than a refund, and it takes time measured in years rather than weeks. So escrow removes one category of risk cleanly and leaves the solvency question exactly where it was, which is why reading the counterparty still matters after you have confirmed the escrow account exists. The mechanics are on the [escrow and Oqood page](/guides/escrow-oqood-dubai-explained/).

## What does the payment schedule tell you?

More than the brochure does, because a schedule is a statement about who needs the money and when. Two plans for similar units can distribute risk very differently.

A schedule weighted toward the early stages asks a buyer to advance a large share of the price before much has been built, which is the shape that hurts most if the project stalls. A schedule weighted toward construction milestones, or one that pushes a meaningful portion past handover, leaves the buyer holding less exposure at every point on the way. Neither shape is proof of anything on its own. What they do is tell you how much of your capital is at risk during the window in which the counterparty question is live, and that is the number to compare across two offers rather than the headline price.

## How should the difference be priced?

As a discount you require, not as a decision you make. This is the practical conclusion and it dissolves most of the argument people have about these two names.

Unobservable counterparty risk has a price. Markets generally pay it, through keener pricing, longer payment schedules, post-handover terms or waived fees, and a buyer receiving those terms is being compensated for something specific. The reasonable position is to know what it is, decide whether the compensation is adequate for your circumstances, and proceed either way. A buyer with capital they cannot afford to have tied up for an extra two years should weigh readability heavily. A buyer with a long horizon and diversified exposure can reasonably take the discount. What nobody should do is take the terms without noticing they are payment for a risk.

## Where does the brand argument actually belong?

At handover and afterwards, where it is a genuinely good argument. Everything above concerns the years before keys, and it is not the whole of what a developer supplies.

Community management, the standard the finished environment is held to, the depth of the resale market a name attracts, and the consistency of what gets built next door are all real and they are all brand-level. Those favour whichever developer has built the deeper, better-managed communities, and that is a legitimate reason to prefer one over the other. It is simply a different argument from the one about who holds your money for three years, and running them together is what produces comparisons that sound thorough and never mention the balance sheet. The wider developer landscape is on the [Dubai developers guide](/guides/dubai-developers-guide/) and the specific failure modes on the [off-plan risks page](/guides/off-plan-risks-delays-dubai/).
